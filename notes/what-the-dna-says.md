# What the DNA says

*September 2026. A granddaughter of Pablo Armando has tested at MyHeritage, and her son shared the
result with this archive. It confirms one thing outright and raises a better question than it answers.*

## First, the privacy line

**The test belongs to a living person**, and this archive omits the living. She is not named here, and
**her full ethnicity profile is not published**. Only three figures are recorded, and only because they
are evidence about **Pablo Armando, who died in 1950**. The rest is held privately.

Her position matters for reading any of it: **she is one quarter Pablo Armando.** Her other three
grandparents are **Mary Septima Taylor** (English, of Devon and Suffolk) and **two Booyzens** (Cape
Afrikaner).

## The thing it settles

> **A high-confidence genetic group: *Argentina (Corrientes and Buenos Aires), Uruguay and Paraguay*.**
> Group 6330, built from **178 DNA kits, 61 of them linked to family trees**.

A genetic group is not an ethnicity percentage. It is a cluster of people who share actual segments of
chromosome and whose documented trees converge on one region. This one converges on the **Río de la
Plata**.

**It can only have come from him.** English Devon and Cape Afrikaner do not produce a Río de la Plata
cluster. Of her four grandparents, exactly one was born in Argentina.

So the Argentine origin now rests on **three independent legs**: his own 1940 passport; the 1908 army
enrolment card found in a drawer in Buenos Aires; and a chromosome. Until a fortnight ago it rested on
one.

**And the group's top places for 1900–1950 name *Santa Fe*.** Not merely Argentina, and not merely
Buenos Aires — **Santa Fe, by name**. That is the first time anything outside the family's own paperwork
has independently pointed at the province the passport and the card both give.

## The thing it questions

Here the result is more interesting than comfortable.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Iberian in her profile** | **0.0%** |
| Italian | **11.1%** — North 6.1, South 3.6, Sardinian 1.4 |

**There is no Spanish component at all.** If Pablo Armando had been the Spanish-descended criollo this
archive has quietly assumed, a granddaughter should carry something in the order of **25% Iberian**. She
carries none.

What she does carry is **Italian** — and her other three grandparents are English and Afrikaner, so it
has nowhere else to come from. At one quarter, **11.1% in her implies roughly 44% in him: about one
Italian parent.**

And the split is **northern-dominant, 6.1% against 3.6%** — the signature of the Ligurian and Piedmontese
emigration that made Rosario the most Italian city in Argentina.

## Why that would explain rather a lot

If **Juan Carlos Lerena or María Lerena was an Italian immigrant**, several stubborn nulls stop being
mysterious:

1. **No Lerena marriage at Rosario, 1867–1885** — read at full resolution and clean. *A couple who married
   in Italy before emigrating would not be in that book at all.*
2. **The two months around his birth, read act by act, are full of Ligurian and Piedmontese names** —
   Piaggio, Ricotti, Savoni, Bianchi, Ferrari, Daneri, Scorzetti, Busaglia, Forchelli, Balerini, Peduci,
   Louraschi, Luraschi, Ranieri, Figallo, Comazzi, Tavella, Angeli. That is the world he was born into.
3. **His death was registered LERINA.** This archive has treated that as a clerk's slip for four weeks.
   But **CEMLA records LERINA as an *Italian* surname** — Laurensa Lerina, 59, and Savina Lerina, 55,
   both *Italiana*, arriving from **Genova** on the *Adelaide Lavarello* in 1889. If the family were
   North Italian, **LERINA may be nearer the original than LERENA**, and the "error" on his death
   certificate may be the one document that got it right.
4. **The Cajón 985 drawer filed the name by sound**, among Lera, Lorena, Serena, Perena — a clerk hearing
   a foreign name.

**None of this is proved.** It is a hypothesis with unusual explanatory reach, and it is written down as
one.

## What this does not do

**Ethnicity estimates are not evidence of the same class as a record.** At one quarter the noise is
large, reference panels bleed into one another, and MyHeritage in particular is known to distribute
Iberian awkwardly across French, North Italian and Sardinian. **A 0% Iberian reading is suggestive; it is
not a disproof of Spanish ancestry.**

The **genetic group** is the stronger of the two signals, because it rests on shared segments and
documented trees rather than on a continental model. And the genetic group says *Río de la Plata* — it
does not say *Italian*.

## What to do next with it

1. **See the other five genetic groups.** Only 3 of 8 are shown at high confidence. If an Italian group
   sits among the other five, that would move this from arithmetic to evidence. *One slider, one
   screenshot.*
2. **Work the DNA matches**, not the percentages. A match with a documented Argentine or Italian tree is
   worth more than any estimate. Filter the match list for Argentine, Uruguayan and Italian trees.
3. **Re-read the sweeps for LERINA**, not only LERENA. The index sweeps recorded no Lerena, Llerena,
   Larena or Lorena — **Lerina was not separately looked for**, and it sorts adjacent.
4. **CEMLA under LERINA and LARENA**, treating them as the primary spellings rather than variants.
