# The Cape Town enrolment card

*Written 10 September 2026. This is the answer to the question the archive was built to ask.*

## What it says

FamilySearch image group **#112342450**, image **665 of 1,119** —
*Fichas de enrolamiento*, **Cajón 985 (Lera)**, creator **Ejército Argentino**.
Image ark `3:1:3QHK-MQL4-NQWN`; the index entry that led to it is `1:1:6TH8-DVXH`.

A printed army enrolment card, filled in by hand:

> **Clase de 1882**
> **LERENA — Pablo Armando**
> hijo de **Juan Carlos Lerena** y de **María Lerena**
> Nacido en **Rosario de Santa Fé**
> Región Militar ⟨blank⟩ · Distrito Militar N.º ⟨blank⟩
> Oficina enroladora de **Capetown** · Matrícula N.º ⟨blank⟩
> Domicilio ⟨blank⟩
> Serie **3885** · Casilla ⟨blank⟩
> Sección **I 3322** · N.º de órden ⟨blank⟩

## Why it is certainly him

Four independent facts on one card, every one of them already known from elsewhere:

1. **Clase de 1882** — his own 1940 passport gives 22 February 1882.
2. **Nacido en Rosario de Santa Fé** — the passport names that town and that province, and it is
   the province that separated the real man from every near-miss this archive has logged.
3. **Oficina enroladora de Capetown** — he died at Newlands, Cape Town, in 1950. An Argentine
   conscript of the class of 1882 enrolling *at the Cape* is not a coincidence; it is a man
   registering with his consulate because he was already living there.
4. The passport's own annotation **Guardia Nacional 1915** is what this card is the paperwork for.

Taken singly any one of these would be suggestive. Together, on one card, in a drawer filed
under **Lera**, they are conclusive.

## And so, the parents

> **Juan Carlos Lerena** and **María Lerena**

That is the line the archive has been chasing since the beginning.

**One caution, stated rather than buried.** The mother is written *María Lerena* — the same
surname as the father. On these cards the formula *hijo de X y de Y* usually gives each parent's
own name, which would make her a Lerena by birth and the marriage one between kin. But some
clerks wrote the mother under her married name. The card alone cannot decide it. Her maiden
name is the next thing to settle, and the Rosario marriage registers — already swept 1868–1891
for the wrong purpose — can now be re-read for the *right* one: a marriage of **Juan Carlos
Lerena**.

## How badly the index had it

FamilySearch's machine index rendered the card as:

| Field | Machine index | The card |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Armand Lorena | **Pablo Armando Lerena** |
| Birthplace | Rosario de Lerma, Salta | **Rosario de Santa Fe** |
| Father | Juan Carlos Lorena Serena | **Juan Carlos Lerena** |
| Mother | María Lerena | María Lerena ✓ |
| Birth date | 1882 | Clase de 1882 ✓ |

The index dropped *Pablo*, moved him 1,400 km to Salta, turned *Lerena* into *Lorena*, and
invented a second surname *Serena* out of the word **Serie** printed on the next line down.
The record page says plainly, "This record was indexed by a computer."

**The lesson is not that the index was bad. It is that the index was nearly right and was
nearly discarded.** The previous round's note called this candidate *"probably not him"* and
listed four objections — Salta, the missing *Pablo*, the surname *Lorena*, the machine
indexing. Three of the four were artefacts of the transcription. Only opening the image
could tell them apart, and opening it took two minutes.

## What follows from the drawer

The volume is **Cajón 985 (Lera)** — the cards are filed alphabetically. Every other Argentine
of enrolment age surnamed **Lerena** is in this same box, within a few images of this one, each
card naming *his* parents. That is a ready-made map of the family, and none of it has been read.

## The rest of the drawer, read the same afternoon

Images **650–688** of the same film were swept through the viewer's Image Index panel — the same
HTML-table trick that made the Rosario registers readable without reading handwriting. Recorded act by
act in `data/cajon-985-scan.tsv`.

### The drawer is filed by sound, not by spelling

**Lera · Lerena · Lorena · Serena · Sera · Era · Leza · Perena · Perella · Hereijo** are all interfiled.
That is not sloppiness in the index — it is how the *army clerk* filed the cards in 1908. It explains
why FamilySearch's machine index produced "Lorena" and "Serena" so readily: the mangled forms were
already sitting in the drawer as neighbours. **The same family appears under five spellings** — Gilberto
and Julia Salvañach's sons are indexed as *Lorena*, *Lerena* and *Perena* on four consecutive cards.

### The negative, stated carefully

Across thirty-nine images, **Pablo Armando is the only Lerena in this drawer born at Rosario de Santa
Fe, and no other son of Juan Carlos Lerena appears.** If he had a brother of enrolment age who
registered, that brother is not here.

**What that does and does not mean.** It does *not* mean he had no brothers. Enrolment abroad was done
at a consulate by a man who chose to present himself; a brother who stayed in Argentina would be filed
in his own district, not in this drawer, and a brother who never enrolled is nowhere at all. The
drawer covers roughly the classes of 1870–1907, so a brother born within a decade of him *would* be of
the right age — but only if he enrolled, and only if his card was filed by the same sound.

It is a real negative all the same, and worth publishing: the first search that could have produced a
sibling produced none.

### Also noted, and set aside

**Victorio Bartolomé Lera, born 1882, Capital, Mendoza** (image 658), whose son Víctor was born in 1902
(image 660). Born the same year as Pablo Armando and filed six cards away — but Mendoza, not Rosario,
and *Lera*, not *Lerena*, with a son already born when Pablo Armando was still unmarried. Recorded so
it is not found again and mistaken for something.
