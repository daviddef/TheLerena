# The consulate that should not have been there

*10 September 2026. A small date, checked while FamilySearch was shut, that changes how two documents
should be read.*

## The standard account

Argentina and South Africa **established diplomatic relations on 10 September 1947**. South Africa opened
a consulate at Buenos Aires in **1938** and a legation in **1948**; **Argentina opened its legation at
Pretoria in 1950**, and both were raised to embassies in 1960. Before 1938 the published account records
nothing at all.

## What this family's papers show

Two documents in this archive, both of them Argentine, both of them official:

| | |
|---|---|
| **1908** | *Ficha de enrolamiento*: **"Oficina enroladora de CAPETOWN"** — an office enrolling Argentine conscripts at the Cape. |
| **1940** | *Pasaporte Argentino* Série A No. 07962, issued by the **"Oficina Consular Argentina en Cape Town, Unión de Sud Africa"**, signed **A. B. Bayne, Encargado, Consulado General Argentino**, under an embossed consular seal, with a **16.50-peso consular stamp**. |

**So Argentina was operating a consular office at Cape Town by 1908 and still in 1940 — thirty-nine years
before it had diplomatic relations with the country, and thirty years before the first consulate the
published histories mention.**

## Why that is not a contradiction, and why it matters anyway

**Consular relations are not diplomatic relations.** States routinely appoint consuls where they have no
legation at all, and the usual instrument is the **honorary consul**: a resident merchant, unpaid,
commissioned to stamp passports, register nationals and witness documents. Such posts predate legations
by decades and leave almost no trace in diplomatic histories, which is exactly why the standard account
is silent.

**But it settles something for this family.** The archive has carried the tradition that Pablo Armando
was **Consul General** as *Disputed* — because on the 1940 passport he is plainly the **bearer**, and the
signature belongs to **A. B. Bayne**. The obvious counter-question has been: *how would such a story
start at all?*

It now has a mechanism. There **was** an Argentine consular office at the Cape, it existed for at least
thirty-two years, and it was almost certainly **honorary and locally staffed** — *A. B. Bayne* is not a
Spanish name. In a city with a handful of Argentine nationals, the man who held the country's passport
and dealt with its consulate would be, in family memory, "the one who was something with the consulate."

> **The story is not proved. It is now explicable, which is a different and more honest thing.**

## Three things this makes worth doing

1. **The Government Gazette exequatur.** A consul needs the host state's formal recognition, and it is
   published. Search the **Cape of Good Hope Government Gazette** before 1910 and the **Union Gazette**
   after, for the Argentine consul at Cape Town — and for **A. B. Bayne**, to date his incumbency. That
   would also produce the names of everyone who held the post across 1900–1950, and it is a short,
   finite, dated search.
2. **The Cancillería request already lodged** just became better aimed. A consular office that small kept
   a **matrícula** — a register of the Argentine nationals in its district. In a city with very few, Pablo
   Armando would be on it, and so might any relative.
3. **The 1908 enrolment itself is a fixed point.** He was at the Cape, and *registering with his own
   government*, in 1908. That is three years after his eldest son's birth there and three years before
   his marriage — and it means he was not hiding from the Argentine state.

## And a footnote against the family lore

The **communist-on-the-run** tradition sits badly with this. A man fleeing Argentina does not walk into
his own country's consular office and put his name, his class, his birthplace and his parents on a
government form. Not proof — a fugitive might well regularise himself abroad, and Ley 4144 deported
*foreigners*, not Argentines. But it is evidence on the other side, and it belongs on the record.

## Also noted in passing

**About 600 Afrikaners emigrated to Argentina in 1902**, after the South African War — the traffic
between these two countries in this exact window ran **both ways**, and neither direction is well
recorded. It sits alongside the remount-trade idea in `requests/ships-and-the-remount-trade.md`, which
remains the best explanation this archive has for how a Rosario horseman reached the Cape.
