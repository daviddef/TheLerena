# Spain — tested, and the name itself argues against it

**Run 9 September 2026** against FamilySearch's Spanish collections: *Spain Baptisms 1502–1940*,
*Spain Catholic Church Records 1307–2005*, *Spain Civil Records 1475–2005*, and the provincial municipal
record sets (Almería, Sevilla, Barcelona, Valencia, Valladolid, Huelva, León, A Coruña, Alicante).

Spain was the last of the four candidate birthplaces. It had a real claim: **Forebears counts 888 Lerenas
in Spain**, the largest absolute total anywhere, and the **CEMLA arrivals cluster hard on Logroño, La
Rioja** — most Spanish Lerenas who sailed for Buenos Aires came from there.

## Result 1 — no Pablo Lerena born in 1882

The whole of Spain yields **one** Pablo Lerena of anything like the right generation:

> **Pablo Timoteo Lerena Muñoz**, born **1878** at San Juan Evangelista, **Bochones, Atienza,
> Guadalajara**; parents **Pedro Lerena Pérez** and **Baltasara Muñoz Bermejos**; married
> **Prudencia Catalina Roldán** at Atienza.

Four years early, and **he stayed in Spain** — he married there. He is not our man.

## Result 2 — and this is the more useful one

***There is no Armando Lerena in Spain at all.*** Not one, in any collection, in any century. Every result
the search returns is fuzzy noise: *Loreno*, *Lorena*, and the eighteenth-century *Inquisición de Llerena*
baptisms from Almendral in Badajoz.

That matters, because **Armando is the name this family actually keeps.** Pablo **Armando**; his son Roque
Luis **Armando**; **Anton Armando** Lerena of the 1980 divorce and the 2013 estate; and — found only
yesterday in Uruguay — **Gregorio Armando Lerena**, marrying at Flores in 1930.

**Armando is a Río de la Plata name, not a Spanish peninsular one of the 1880s.** It came into Spanish
usage through Italian influence, which is precisely the demographic signature of Buenos Aires, Rosario and
Montevideo in the late nineteenth century — cities remade by Italian immigration — and not of rural La
Rioja or Guadalajara. The Rosario sweep is consistent with this: across **three whole years** of that index
there were only **two Armandos** — rare, but present. In Spain, across four centuries of indexed records,
**none**.

**So the name points away from Spain and back across the Atlantic.** That is not proof, but it is the first
piece of evidence in this project that discriminates *between* the candidate origins rather than merely
failing to confirm one.

## Result 3 — the Spanish Lerenas are scattered, not a kindred
Unlike Uruguay, where eleven children of six brothers sit in one small town, the Spanish bearers are spread
thin across the whole country — Almería, Sevilla, Écija, Barcelona, Valencia, Sueca, Valladolid, Marcilla
in Navarra, Atienza in Guadalajara, Huelva, León, Ferrol. And in most of them **Lerena is the *second*
surname** — the maternal one: *Manuel Fernández Lerena*, *Teresa Fernández Lerena*, *Juan García Lerena*,
*Amparo Arrieles Lerena*. It behaves like a name being absorbed into other families, not one founding them.

## A point about the passport that now carries more weight
**Spanish practice gives every person two surnames**, paternal then maternal. **Pablo Armando's 1940
Argentine passport carries one.** That has been noted here before as odd; against the Spanish evidence it
becomes odder still, and it fits better with either Río de la Plata informal usage or with the
mother's-surname/illegitimacy reading already on the hypotheses page.

## The honest limit
This is weaker as a negative than the Uruguayan test. **Spanish parish registers are enormous and only
patchily indexed**, and diocesan archives hold far more than FamilySearch has keyed. An 1882 baptism in an
unindexed Riojan parish would not surface here. What can fairly be said is: **he is not in the indexed
Spanish records, and the given name his family carried does not belong to Spain.**

## A footnote worth keeping — where the name comes from
**Llerena is a town in Badajoz, Extremadura**, and the surname *Llerena* is toponymic from it; the town was
important enough to host a tribunal of the Inquisition, whose registers surfaced in these very searches.
*Lerena* with one L is the commoner form in the Río de la Plata. This does not tell us where **our** family
came from, but it does explain why the two spellings have shadowed each other through every search in this
project.
