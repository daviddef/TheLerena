# The story that he fled Argentina as a political refugee

**Family testimony, 9 September 2026.** David reports a story carried in the family and repeated to him by
his mother, Cheryl Anne Lerena, the night before: that **Pablo Armando "ran away from Argentina because he
was a communist on the run."** Never proven, never documented, but persistent — and it has now survived
three generations, which is worth something in itself.

## Why this is worth taking seriously rather than filing under folklore

It is the **only story the family carries that explains a departure**. Everything else the family remembers
about him — Rosario, the horses, the consulship, the ostriches — describes what he *was*. This is the only
one that says why he *left*. And a reason to leave is exactly the thing the documentary record has refused
to supply.

It also fits, uncomfortably well, with the shape of the evidence:

- **He is not in the Rosario baptism index.** Twenty-six letter sections read for 1882 under every surname,
  and now 1881 and 1883 under way. A man who left under a political cloud is precisely the sort of man
  whose paper trail is thin at the origin — or whose name at the origin is not the name he later used.
- **He used at least three names**: *Pablo Armando Lerena* on the Argentine passport, *Robert Paul Lerena*
  in South African civil records, *Bob Lerena* in the racing yard. That is a lot of names for one man.
- **His passport carries one surname where Spanish practice ordinarily gives two.**
- **He was issued an Argentine passport in 1940**, and had to prove nationality to get it — so whatever the
  circumstances of his leaving, he was not stateless and was not permanently barred.

## The chronology, and what it points at

Pablo Armando was born (per the passport) **22 February 1882**. His eldest son Roque was born at **Cape
Town in November 1905**. So he left Argentina, and arrived in South Africa, at roughly **age 21 to 23,
somewhere in 1903–1905**.

That is a very specific window, and it is one of the most turbulent in Argentine labour history.

### The correction the story needs
**"Communist" is almost certainly a later relabelling.** The *Partido Comunista* did not exist in Argentina
until **1918**. In 1903–1905 the radical movement in Argentina was overwhelmingly **anarchist and
anarcho-syndicalist** — the FORA, founded 1901 — with a smaller socialist party. Families routinely
retrofit "communist" onto any earlier left-wing politics once that becomes the available word. So the
story should be read as **"he was in political trouble on the left and had to get out"**, which is a
different claim from the literal one, and a far more plausible one.

### The mechanism that would make it true
**Ley 4144, the *Ley de Residencia*, passed November 1902.** It let the Argentine government **deport any
foreign-born person deemed a threat to national security, without trial and without appeal.** It was
enacted directly in response to the general strike of 1902 and used heavily against anarchist organisers in
the years that follow — exactly the 1903–1905 window.

**And Rosario is not incidental to this.** Rosario was Argentina's great river port and rail head, with a
large dock and railway workforce, and it was one of the most militant anarchist cities in the country. A
young man in political trouble in Rosario between 1902 and 1905 is a completely ordinary historical figure.

### *** The part that may matter most ***
**The Ley de Residencia applied to FOREIGNERS.** An Argentine national could be prosecuted but not
deported under it. So if the story is true in its strongest form — that he was *expelled* — then
**he was not Argentine-born**, and the failure to find him in the Rosario baptism register stops being a
puzzle and becomes a consequence.

That would sit with several things already on the table: the **Uruguayan** density of the surname (the
highest in the world), the Uruguayan-born Lerenas in the 1895 Buenos Aires census, the Uruguayan
**Carlos Lerena b. c.1885** in the CEMLA arrivals, and the family note that the consulship may have been
**Uruguayan rather than Argentine**.

If instead he merely *fled* prosecution rather than being deported, he could still be Argentine-born — and
the passport of 1940 would be straightforwardly his own.

## How this could actually be tested

1. **Ministerio del Interior deportation and expulsion files**, Archivo General de la Nación, Buenos Aires.
   Ley 4144 expulsions generated named case files. This is the direct test.
2. **Policía de Rosario / Santa Fe provincial police records** for 1902–1905, and the *Orden Social*
   political-police holdings where they survive.
3. **The anarchist press** — *La Protesta* (Buenos Aires) and Rosario's own labour papers — which named
   arrested and deported militants. Much is digitised at the International Institute of Social History in
   Amsterdam, which holds the major Argentine anarchist collections.
4. **British and South African arrival records for 1903–1905**, which is where he must appear if he came by
   sea. Note that the family also carries the story that he met Mary Septima Taylor **on a ship**, and she
   was in **England** — so a route Argentina → Britain → South Africa is not as odd as it first looks if he
   was leaving in a hurry and taking the passage he could get.
5. **The 1940 passport application itself** — the *matrícula consular* at the Argentine foreign ministry
   archive, already on the roadmap. Whatever he produced in Cape Town in 1940 to prove nationality is the
   single document most likely to name his parents and birthplace.

## Status
**Family lore. Untested. Not cited as fact anywhere on this site.** Recorded here because it is
falsifiable, because it explains a gap nothing else explains, and because it points at named archives that
have not yet been asked.
