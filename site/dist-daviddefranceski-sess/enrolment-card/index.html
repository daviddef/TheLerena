The enrolment card

An Argentine army enrolment card, filed in a drawer marked Lera, gives the class of 1882, a birth at Rosario de Santa Fe, an enrolling office at Cape Town — and the names of Pablo Armando Lerena's father and mother.

And what the index made of it

Follow this one and you will not find Pablo Armando Lerena. You will find “Armand Lorena”, born 1882 at “Rosario de Lerma, Salta” — a real town, seven hundred miles from the one on the card. No search for Lerena at Santa Fe could ever have returned it.

It is also attached to a FamilySearch tree person, GPWH-72S, “Pablo Armando Larena, 1882–1950”. Whose attachment that is has not been examined.

What this card actually is

It is worth saying plainly, because the obvious reading of a military document is the wrong one here. This is not a record of army service, and it does not show a man being sent anywhere.

It is a printed card with blank rules, filled in by hand. It is not a birth record and does not pretend to be one: it is the paperwork of a man of the class of 1882 registering for Argentine military service at the consulate in Cape Town. That is precisely what makes it useful. Everything on it was stated by someone who knew — almost certainly by Pablo Armando himself.

It is a conscription registration, and every Argentine man had to make one. Argentina introduced compulsory military service in 1901, and from then on men were enrolled in a clase named for the year they were born. So Clase de 1882 is not a date on this card — it is his cohort. It says he was born in 1882, which his passport says too, and it says nothing whatever about when he sat down and filled this in. (The law and the clase system are background, not something this archive has sourced from a record. Everything else on this page is.)

And then look at what is blank, because the blanks are the evidence. Región Militar: nothing. Distrito Militar N.º: nothing. A man enrolling in Argentina is placed in a military region and given a district — that is the whole administrative point of enrolling. He has neither. What he has instead is Oficina enroladora de Capetown, and for an Argentine abroad that meant one thing: he registered at his own consulate.

So the card is the reverse of what it looks like. It is not an Argentine soldier arriving at the Cape. It is a man who was already living at the Cape, doing his Argentine paperwork there — and that is precisely why it is useful to this archive. A conscript card filed in Cape Town is documentary evidence that he had emigrated before he filled it in.

The rest of the blanks say the same thing. Matrícula N.º, Domicilio, Casilla and N.º de órden are all empty. He registered; he was never mustered. The only fields carrying numbers are the filing ones, Serie E 3555 and Sección I 3322 — how the army stored the card, not anything about him.

And the one line that no other document in the world supplies: hijo de Juan Carlos Lerena y de María Lerena. His death notice of 1950 says “Father: not known. Mother: not known.” This card is the answer to that, and it is the reason the archive exists. What it does not settle about his mother is set out below.

One caution: the card does not date itself

This page and others call it the ficha of 1908. Nothing on the card says 1908 — the only year on its face is the class, 1882 — and this archive has not written down where the 1908 came from. It sits in a FamilySearch collection catalogued 1911–1936.

Set against that, page 6 of his passport records his libreta de enrolamiento issued by the Consulado General Argentino, Cape Town, with Dist. Militar a dash, Matrícula a dash, and Año 1915. That is the same pattern as this card, down to the two dashes. So either there were two Argentine enrolment events at the Cape, or the card and the libreta belong to one — and if they do, the date on this card is 1915, not 1908. It is flagged rather than decided, because the date is what one would cite for when he was already in South Africa, and that is worth being right about.

Why it is certainly him

Four things on this one card were already known from elsewhere, from documents that have nothing to do with the Argentine army:

  1. The class of 1882. His own passport gives 22 February 1882.
  2. Nacido en Rosario de Santa Fé. The passport names that town and that province — and the province is the thing that separated the real man from every near miss this archive has logged.
  3. Oficina enroladora de Capetown. He died at Newlands, Cape Town, in 1950. An Argentine conscript enrolling at the Cape is not a coincidence; it is a man registering with his consulate because he already lived there.
  4. The passport's own annotation — Guardia Nacional, 6º de Caballería, 1915 — is the service this card is the enrolment for.

Any one of them alone would be suggestive. Together, on one card, in a drawer filed under Lera, they are conclusive.

And from the other side of the ocean

Pablo Armando's eldest son — named with his exact birth date in the 1950 liquidation account — is Ricardo Juan Carlos Lerena, born 6 January 1920 at Cape Town. A grandson of the same names died in infancy in the Transvaal.

An Argentine army card and a South African deceased estate, neither with any possible knowledge of the other, name the same man. The grandfather's name came out of Argentina with him and was given to his first son at the Cape.

How nearly this was thrown away

The index that led here was made by a computer, and it was wrong in four places out of five.

The index dropped Pablo, moved him 1,400 km to Salta, turned Lerena into Lorena, and invented a second surname — “Serena” — out of the word SERIE printed on the line below the parents.

This archive had already found the entry and had already written it off. The note from the previous round called it “probably not him” and listed four objections: Salta, the missing Pablo, the surname Lorena, and the machine indexing. Three of the four were artefacts of the transcription.

The rule that follows. An index entry is a pointer, not a fact. Where a candidate has been excluded on indexed detail alone, the exclusion is worth nothing until the image is opened. Several exclusions in this archive rest on exactly that, and are now marked for re-checking.

What the card does not settle

María Lerena is written with her husband's surname. On these cards the formula hijo de X y de Y usually gives each parent's own name — which would make her a Lerena by birth, and the marriage one between kin. But some clerks entered the mother under her married name. The card cannot decide it, and neither can this archive. Her maiden name is the next thing to find.

And a genuine puzzle now sharpens. The Rosario baptism index has been read letter by letter, under any surname, for 1881, 1882 and 1883 — seventy-eight letter-bands — and it contains no Lerena at all. A boy born in Rosario in February 1882 to a father named Lerena is not in the cathedral's index. Either he was baptised somewhere the index does not reach, or under a spelling the sweep did not catch, or not baptised as an infant. That is now a specific question rather than a blank. The rest of the drawer

Roughly forty frames either side of the card were swept the same afternoon — the section opens with a hand-lettered guide tab reading “Lere. n.” immediately before Pablo Armando. The result is a negative, and it is published as one.

The drawer is filed by sound, not by spelling. Lera, Lerena, Lorena, Serena, Sera, Era, Leza, Perena, Perella and Hereijo are all interfiled — not by FamilySearch, but by the army clerk in 1908. That is why the machine index produced “Lorena” and “Serena” so readily: the mangled forms were already sitting in the drawer as neighbours. One family — Gilberto Lerena’s — appears under five different spellings on five cards.

Entre Ríos, Mendoza, Buenos Aires, Montevideo. Not one of them is from Rosario de Santa Fe, and not one father is Juan Carlos. Across thirty-nine images, Pablo Armando is the only Lerena in the drawer born at Rosario, and no other son of Juan Carlos Lerena appears.

What that does not mean: that he had no brothers. Enrolment abroad was done at a consulate by a man who presented himself. A brother who stayed in Argentina would be filed in his own military district, not in this drawer; a brother who never enrolled is nowhere at all. But the drawer covers the classes of roughly 1870–1907, so a brother born within a decade of him would have been of the right age — and the first search that could have produced a sibling produced none.

What it opens

The drawer has been read and is written up above. What it opens is elsewhere: a marriage record for Juan Carlos Lerena, which would give María her maiden name in a single line; the consular matrícula at Cape Town, where a man who enrolled in 1908 had a file, and which has already been requested from the Argentine foreign ministry; and the missing baptism at Rosario.
