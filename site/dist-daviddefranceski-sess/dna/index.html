A third leg under the Argentine origin — and a better question

The DNA

A granddaughter of Pablo Armando has tested at MyHeritage. One result confirms outright what until a fortnight ago rested on a single passport. The other unsettles an assumption this archive had been making without noticing.

On privacy, first. The test belongs to a living person, and this archive omits the living. She is not named on this page and her ethnicity profile is not published. Three figures appear below, and only because they are evidence about Pablo Armando, who died in 1950. The rest is held privately. Method →

Her position in the descent is what makes any of it readable: she is one quarter Pablo Armando. Her other three grandparents are Mary Septima Taylor — English, of Devon and Suffolk — and two Booyzens, Cape Afrikaner.

What it settles

Genetic group · confidence: high

Argentina (Corrientes and Buenos Aires), Uruguay and Paraguay

Group 6330 · built from 178 DNA kits, 61 of them linked to family trees

A genetic group is not an ethnicity percentage. It is a cluster of people who share actual segments of chromosome and whose documented trees converge on one region. This one converges on the Río de la Plata.

It can only have come from him. English Devon and Cape Afrikaner do not produce a Río de la Plata cluster. Of her four grandparents, exactly one was born in Argentina.

So the Argentine origin now stands on three independent legs: his own 1940 passport; the 1908 army enrolment card found in a drawer in Buenos Aires; and a chromosome. A fortnight ago it stood on one.

And the group’s top places for 1900–1950 name Santa Fe. Not merely Argentina, and not merely Buenos Aires — Santa Fe, by name, alongside Buenos Aires, Corrientes and Entre Ríos. That is the first time anything outside this family’s own paperwork has independently pointed at the province the passport and the card both give.

There is no Spanish component whatever — at MyHeritage. If Pablo Armando had been the Spanish-descended criollo this archive has quietly assumed, a granddaughter should carry something in the order of 25% Iberian. MyHeritage gives her none; 23andMe gives her 2.1%, which is also far short of 25%, so the shape of the argument survives even though the flat zero does not.

What she does carry is Italian — and her other three grandparents are English and Afrikaner, so it has nowhere else to come from. At one quarter, 11.1% in her implies roughly 44% in him: about one Italian parent.

And at MyHeritage the split is northern-dominant, 6.1% against 3.6% — which this archive read as the signature of the Ligurian and Piedmontese emigration that made Rosario the most Italian city in Argentina. 23andMe points the other way and names Sicily. Two panels agree that the Italian is there and disagree about which end of Italy it came from, so the northern reading is withdrawn as a DNA argument. The Ligurian names in the Rosario register are still in the Rosario register; they were never a DNA claim.

The other groups — and they weaken the Italian case

Same day. The slider was moved to low confidence and the count went from three of eight to eight of eight. The result argues against the hypothesis above, and it is recorded here rather than left in a drawer.

A technical point first: the confidence slider governs the genetic groups only. The ethnicity percentages are identical in both views — the same thirteen values, the same figures. Nothing above changes.

No Italian genetic group appears among the five. That is evidence against the Italian reading, and it arrived within an hour of the hypothesis being written down.

It does not kill it — a genetic group needs enough tested people from a region with documented trees, and an Italian great-grandparent’s cluster can fail to form. But the easiest confirmation was available and did not appear, and the case is weaker for it than it was this morning.

The asymmetry is worth stating plainly. The Río de la Plata group is a positive result: a cluster formed, at high confidence, from 178 kits. The Italian claim rests on a negative — an absence of Iberian percentage — plus arithmetic on an 11.1% figure. Those are not the same class of evidence, and this archive should not treat them as though they were.

And one thing the eight groups do confirm. Every group except the Río de la Plata one is accounted for by her other three grandparents — two South African, two British Isles. That is what a clean four-grandparent reading looks like, and it makes the Argentine group harder to explain away rather than easier. The thing it proves, it proves more firmly than before.

The one thing two companies agree on

Italian — 11.1% at MyHeritage, 8.0% at 23andMe

Two laboratories, two reference panels, one signal, in a woman whose other three grandparents are English and Cape Afrikaner. This is now the best-supported thing in the whole DNA file — better supported than the 0% Iberian it was originally hung on, because that figure turned out to be one company’s opinion and this one did not.

At a quarter, 8% in her implies roughly 32% in him; MyHeritage’s figure implies 44%. Somewhere between one Italian parent and one Italian grandparent. The error bars on a quarter are large and that range is the honest width of them.

And one thing that is behind a paywall. 23andMe will say which parent each slice of ancestry came from — it is called Parental Inheritance, and it does not need a parent to have tested. It would say outright whether the Italian sits on her father’s side, where Pablo Armando is, or her mother’s, where the Cape is. That is the question this entire page argues around, and one screen would answer it. It requires 23andMe+ Premium, and this archive does not buy sources. It is on the work list as David’s.

Eleven thousand cousins, and not one of them is a Lerena

The ethnicity estimates are models. Matches are people. On 16 September 2026 her match lists were read properly for the first time — 11,581 at MyHeritage, of which 11,114 have a family tree attached, and 1,500 at 23andMe. 6,350 of the MyHeritage matches were pulled down to 13.78 cM, which covers everything this archive would act on.

A null is worth nothing until something known returns in the same form. The one South American family, read

There is exactly one. A Uruguayan family — three kits sharing a single tree of 112 people. The eldest of the three shares 21.4 cM across two segments, largest 11.3; MyHeritage rates him a fifth cousin at medium confidence, and reports that the ancestral places common to him and her include Argentina. They are living and are not named here. Their tree was opened and read.

It is Basque-Navarrese and French-Basque, settled in Uruguay: Ciganda, Ziganda Erviti, De Goni, Rodriguez Salvatierra, Pereyra, Barneche, Larralde, Iraola, Echeverria, Cha, Arnaldi, Giriboni, Larrieu, Laclau, Perrone — back to Francisco Ziganda Erviti; and Pedro Maria Ciganda, 1822-1882. Those people are long dead, which is why they can be named here when the living cannot.

There is no Lerena in it. No Llerena, no Lenguas, no Traibel, no Aguilar, no Fernández. It is a real Río de la Plata connection and it does not corroborate the hypothesis. At 21 cM on a largest segment of 11 it is also close enough to the noise floor that this archive would not build on it either way.

And then the clusters came back

David generated MyHeritage AutoClusters on three kits the same evening. The tool groups a person’s matches into blocks that all match each other as well as the tester — so each block is, in practice, one ancestral couple’s descendants. It is the sharpest instrument either company offers, and it needs no interpretation from this archive to be read.

The control — and it is her son’s chart, not hers

His father is a Defranceski, of Istria and Croatia. His chart contains TWO plainly Croatian clusters - Butorac, Pavelich, Jagustin, Krkljes, Racic, Glas - sitting beside the Cape ones.

AutoClusters demonstrably forms a distinct block for a foreign paternal line WHEN COUSINS FROM THAT LINE HAVE TESTED. The method is not blind to a non-Cape father. It found his.

The nephew’s chart, which nobody expected

That is his MOTHER's family, not the Lerena line, and an endogamous population throws up far more high-centimorgan matches than an outbred one, so it crowds his chart out.

He shares exactly 3 clustered matches with his aunt. All three of the matches he shares with her sit in ONE of her clusters - the Cape one that holds Butler, Daniel and Daschner.

The only other tested Lerena contributes three clustered matches to this question, and all three are Cape. This is also the clearest statement yet of why he was never going to separate the lines: he is her NEPHEW, so the Lerena half of him arrives filtered through the same Cape grandmother.

The limit, stated before anyone else states it. AutoClusters only works on matches above a threshold - hers begins at 50.6 cM. A cluster cannot form where nobody has tested, and it cannot form from matches below the floor. The Croatian clusters on her son's chart exist because Croatians test in numbers. The absence of an Argentine cluster on hers is consistent with Pablo Armando's family having no living tested descendants in either database - which is exactly what the sixty-two-country match census already said, where Argentina did not appear at all.

This is a strong negative and it is not a disproof. It says the answer is not reachable by DNA matching today. It does not say the hypothesis is wrong.

The subtraction — and it finds a paternal cluster

Her maternal aunt's chart was generated too. The aunt is Cape and nothing else, so any cluster of the niece's that has no counterpart on the aunt's belongs to her FATHER - which is to say to Pablo Armando or to Mary Septima Taylor. This is the instrument this page has been asking for all day.

The trap first, because this archive nearly fell into it. Run naively it says five of her fourteen clusters are paternal. That is wrong, and the reason is a floor mismatch: the aunt's chart begins at 75.3 cM and the niece's at 50.6. Four of those five clusters have no member above 75.3 cM at all, so they COULD NOT have appeared on the aunt's chart whatever their ancestry. The test can only speak about the 15 of her 107 clustered matches that clear the aunt's floor.

So what does all that do to the case?

It pulls in two directions, and both have to be said.

Stronger: the Italian. It now rests on two companies rather than on one company’s zero. That is a different class of evidence from where it stood this morning.

Weaker: the Uruguayan trunk. If Pablo Armando descended from Sancho Llerena’s Montevideo family — eight children born 1793–1815, and a visible nineteenth-century presence in the city — then somewhere among 11,581 matches with 11,114 trees, and 1,500 more at another company, a cousin of that family might be expected to appear. None does.

And the clusters put that in its hardest form. Her fourteen clusters are exhaustively Cape and British. Her son’s chart, run the same night, throws up two Croatian clusters for his father — so the tool plainly does find a foreign paternal line when one has tested. It found his father’s. It did not find hers.

And the honest counterweight to that. Twenty countries of grandparent-birth among 1,500 relatives, and none of them South American, is not really a fact about this family — it is a fact about who buys DNA tests. Uruguay and Argentina are barely present in either database. An absence measured in a place where nobody is standing is a weak absence, and this page will not pretend otherwise.

What would actually settle it is not more searching. It is Parental Inheritance, which would put the Italian on one side of her or the other; and a Rosario baptism for Pablo Armando naming his father. One costs a subscription. The other is in a Córdoba parish register that is not filmed.

What it does not do

An ethnicity estimate is not evidence of the same class as a record. At one quarter the noise is large, reference panels bleed into one another, and MyHeritage in particular is known to distribute Iberian awkwardly across French, North Italian and Sardinian. A 0% Iberian reading is suggestive. It is not a disproof of Spanish ancestry.

The genetic group is much the stronger of the two signals, because it rests on shared segments and documented trees rather than on a continental model. And note what it actually says: it says Río de la Plata. It does not say Italian.

What to do with it next

  1. See the other five genetic groups. Only three of eight are shown at high confidence. If an Italian group sits among the other five, this moves from arithmetic to evidence. One slider, one screenshot.
  2. Work the matches, not the percentages. A single match with a documented Argentine or Italian tree is worth more than any estimate.
  3. Re-read the sweeps for LERINA. The Rosario index sweeps recorded no Lerena, Llerena, Larena or Lorena — Lerina was never separately looked for, and it sorts adjacent.
  4. CEMLA under LERINA and LARENA, treated as primary spellings rather than variants.
