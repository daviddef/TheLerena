Two Lerena families, two Spanish origins, and neither of them is his

Uruguay

There are two documented Lerena families in nineteenth-century Uruguay, and on 17 September 2026 a baptism act of 1812 settled what they are to each other: nothing. One came from Córdoba in Andalusia, the other from Valdemoro outside Madrid. The surname entered the Banda Oriental twice, from opposite ends of Spain.

Why this page exists. The surname is rare, so it was natural to assume the Uruguayan Lerenas were one kindred. They are two, and both are documented from parish and civil registers rather than from anybody’s tree. Keeping them apart is not pedantry: it is the difference between a family tree and a wish. The two houses

Sancho’s house — Canelones, then Montevideo

SANCHO LLERENA married María Fernández at Canelones on 8 January 1791. The act calls him natural de Córdova la Llana and names his parents, Félix Llerena and Rosalía Aguilar. A dragoon; in 1798 he swore an oath and could not sign his name.

Eight children between 1793 and 1815, and out of them the Montevideo trunk — the Lerena Lenguas and the Lerena Traibel. His son Avelino (1815–1890) married Ambrosia Traibel y Centeno and fathered the second of those branches.

Ramón’s house — Rosario, then the interior

RAMÓN SERENA — so the act writes him; the indexes say Lerena and Gerena — natural de Valdemoro en Castilla la Nueva, married María Josefa González, natural de la Ysla de Canarias, Lanzarote. He was fathering children from about 1800, and his sons were born in Uruguay: Alejandro at Rosario, Mauricio at Colla — the Rosario district of Colonia.

His own parents are Salvador Serena and Ana de Cubas, which is where the current tree’s otherwise unexplained “Ramón Llerena Cubas” comes from. From his sons come the Trinidad kindred of Flores and a spread across San José, Florida, Salto and Montevideo.

They are not one family, and an act of 1812 says so in five words

This was the question worth asking, because if the two houses were one the Uruguayan picture would close. They are not, and the answer did not come from arithmetic in the end. It came from a sentence.

On 15 November 1812, at the Iglesia Matriz of Montevideo, Don Juan Lloberas baptised Manuel Lorenzo, born the day before, and wrote down where the parents came from:

…hijo legítimo de RAMON SERENA natural de VALDEMORO en CASTILLA LA NUEVA, y de María Josefa González natural de la Ysla de Canarias, LANZAROTE. Abuelos paternos Salvador Serena y Ana de Cubas; id. maternos Marcial de León González è Ysavel Lorenza.

Valdemoro is twenty-seven kilometres south of Madrid. Sancho was natural de Córdova la Llana, in Andalusia, five hundred kilometres away. Two separate Spanish emigrations, two separate arrivals, one rare surname. Read the act →

The arithmetic had already said they could not be father and son — Alejandro married in 1825 so Ramón was born about 1775–85, while Sancho married in 1791 and had his first child in 1793 — and their fathers were different men, Félix and Salvador. The act closes what the arithmetic left open.

A coincidence, correctly distrusted, and now discharged

Before the act was read, this page recorded that Sancho’s father is written Feliz GERENA on a Montevideo act of 1811 and Ramón’s father Salvador GERENA on one of 1812 — and called it “a scribal habit of one place and one time rather than two families independently choosing the same variant”, a reason to look and not a reason to believe.

That was exactly right. The act itself writes SERENA, and both men were baptising children at the same font, one year apart. Two families of the same rare surname, in one small city, at one church — and from opposite ends of Spain. The resemblance was real and it meant nothing.

The two descents, drawn

The same four generations of each, side by side. Both begin with a Spanish couple named on a River Plate act and found in no Spanish register yet — so generation one of each is marked as the joint that cannot bear weight. Both cross the Atlantic, both spread across the Banda Oriental, and they never touch.

Why it is drawn to the same scale

Both Uruguayan houses run four generations and reach back to a named couple in Spain. Pablo Armando’s runs two, and stops at a pair of names on one card.

That is not a gap in the research. It is the whole problem, and it is why the Uruguayan work is done in parallel and in hope rather than as his line. Nothing yet connects the left of this page to the right of it.

Five hundred kilometres apart in Spain, and about ninety in Uruguay. Sancho married at Canelones and Ramón baptised at the Iglesia Matriz of Montevideo — one year apart, in the same small city, at the same font — having come from opposite ends of the same country.

The Trinidad nest

At Trinidad, the small inland capital of Flores, six or seven Lerena men were fathering children between 1871 and 1895 — Bernabé, Vitalino, Ysaac, Cipriano, Fermín, Florencio. Two of them are now placed: Bernabé is Alejandro’s son and Cipriano is Mauricio’s, which makes them first cousins.

The mothers are the other finding, and they are Basque — Beloqui, Echeverry, Pelaez, Gazo, Barragán. A Lerena kindred marrying Basque women in one small town speaks to the open question of where the surname comes from; it is evidence about the community they married into, not about the etymology, and it is recorded as that and nothing more.

And they married each other. Lerenas and Pelaezes at Trinidad intermarried across at least forty years, until by 1885 the church required a dispensation for consanguinity to let them do it again — twice in the third degree, once in the second and third accumulated. These are not two families who happened to meet. They are one intermarried kindred in a small inland town.

What would join the two houses, and what would join either of them to Pablo Armando

The houses will not be joined. That question is closed: Córdoba and Valdemoro are five hundred kilometres apart and the act is explicit. What is now open instead is Valdemoro, which is a finite and searchable place. Salvador Serena and Ana de Cubas were married in Spain, and that marriage is findable; so is Ramón’s own baptism, about 1775–85. The archive has a hard Spanish place-name for this family where an hour ago it had none.

And a second stream opens with it that nobody suspected: María Josefa González was a canaria, from Lanzarote. The Canary Islands fed their own migration into the River Plate, and no part of this archive has ever looked at it.

And now the thing this page must not be allowed to obscure. Neither house reaches Pablo Armando. There is no Juan Carlos Lerena in either family, in any generation, in any of the searches that built this page. He was born at Rosario de Santa Fe in Argentina in 1882, and the Argentine end says the same thing from three directions: the cathedral baptism index holds no Lerena for 1881–83, the 1895 census holds no Lerena or Lerina household anywhere in Santa Fe province, and his own parents are named on exactly one document — his enrolment card — and nowhere else on earth.

So these two families are being built in parallel, and in hope. The hope is reasonable: the surname is rare, the River Plate is one world with a river through the middle of it, and the DNA says the Argentine and Uruguayan hypotheses are genetically the same hypothesis. The hope is not evidence, and nothing here is written as though it were.

Sancho’s household — eleven years at Canelones, and four people it owned

Documented The count of Sancho’s children has moved twice, and the file that held it was once called complete. An earlier list held four children under that name; the reconciled list supersedes it and names eight, born between 1793 and 1815. The padrón of 1812 returns six in the household, which is not a contradiction: two were born after it, and José Loreto, born 1806, is in no tree and no census and was found only because the surname was searched as LORENA. The likeliest reading of his absence from the padrón is that he died before 1812 — but no burial has been looked for, and none is claimed.

Documented They lived at Canelones; they did not visit it. José Loreto’s baptism of 11 December 1806 calls Sancho and María “vecinos de esta Villa” — residents. That joins Agustina’s baptism there in 1795, Dominga’s in 1804, and Justa Pastora being called natural de Canelones in 1831. The family lived at Canelones for at least eleven years, and had moved to Montevideo, calle San Juan Nº 60, by the padrón of 1812. The godfather was fetched from Montevideo, which is the other direction.

Inferred And the household explains a name. The act of 30 August 1795 at Canelones reads “bautize, puse el Santo Oleo y Crisma a AGUSTINA RAMONA, DE TRES DIAS NACIDA, hija legitima de D.n Sancho Lerena y de D.a Maria Fernandez, vecinos de la Villa” — which confirms the birth of 27 August 1795 that this register had carried from a tree and a census, and gives her a second forename. Her godfather was Don Fidel Rey, vecino de Montevideo. Four years later her younger brother was christened FIDEL — a forename that appears nowhere else in this family. This family names children for whoever stands at the font, five times over in the acts read here, so the likeliest source of the Lerena Fidel is that man. It is an inference from a pattern and not from a record.

Documented It also settles nothing about the other Fidel. A Montevideo death registration of 1889 names “Abuelos paternos FIDEL LERENA y MARIA RUEDAS”, and that plate was opened on 21 September. Whether that Fidel is Sancho’s son remains unestablished, and nothing on the plate bears on it. The tree’s death of 1822 — the one thing that would have kept them apart — is not in the record either. The two are still not merged: an unevidenced death is not evidence of survival.

The padrón counted four enslaved people in that house and named none of them.

Six weeks later, at the Montevideo Catedral on 26 September 1812, an act names one: “bauticé solemnemente a MARIA MERCEDES, que nació antes de ayer, hija de JUANA, negra esclava de D.n Sancho Lerena y de D.a María Fernández: madrina Catalina Martínez.”

Juana — held by Sancho and María jointly, as the act says and the padrón of the same month confirms. No age, no origin, no surname, no later record. Her daughter María Mercedes, born 24 September 1812 and baptised at two days old. Nothing is known of what became of her.

That plate was opened for one reason. The row had rested on a machine transcript, and a machine is worst at proper nouns — while this woman’s name is the only thing any record gives her. So it was the one that most needed a human to read it. It is JUANA, in the priest’s own hand, film 007713375 image 02628, folio 136. This archive carries her name rather than a number.

Documented The same opening carries a trap this archive walked past. The act immediately before hers baptises another María Mercedes, on 25 September 1812 — “hija de TERESA ABELLEYRA, negra esclava de D.n RAMON ABELLEYRA”. Two enslaved girls of the same name, in two households, on consecutive days, on one page. And this register also holds a Teresa enslaved in Sancho’s household, from the padrón — a different woman, from a different document. The two must not be run together.

When was Sancho last alive? Fourteen records, and only four count

Lerena Villademoros — a fork the archive refused, and then decided

On 16 September a sweep of Sancho’s children forward turned up a branch this archive had never named: children indexed under the compound LERENA VILLADEMOROS, and an Avelino Eduardo Lerena married to Celedonia Villademoros.

It set out two readings and deliberately chose neither. Either Avelino Lerena Fernández, born 1815, married twice — Ambrosia Traibel and then Celedonia — or Avelino Eduardo was a son of his, which would make the Villademoros children Sancho’s great-grandchildren rather than his grandchildren. Documented It is the son. Two parish acts give Avelino Eduardo’s own father as Avelino Serena/Lerena, with Ambrosia Traibel named on the record. So he belongs beside Alejandro, Josefa, Ana Rosario, Alfredo Justiniano and Mercedes as a child of that marriage, and Celedonia is a daughter-in-law.

It is blank, and so is the maternal one. That is common on these forms and is not evidence that he could not have answered.

Documented What the two acts did give is a route. He is viudo on both — a widower at forty-two, while Julia Bassani is twenty-two and soltera, living at a different address, where both children were born. So there is an earlier marriage and an earlier death in Montevideo, and a marriage act names the groom’s parents. He is also an escribano, a notary, and notaries leave protocolos filed under their own names. Those are the two things left to try.

Documented And one row on this site was two men. The register carried a bare “Hector Lerena” of Buenos Aires who married Amalia Pacheco. His marriage act of 25 February 1911 at San Carlos was opened: “hijo legítimo de D. Alejandro Máximo … y de doña Julia Ba[ssani], natural de Italia … con doña Amalia Pa[checo]”, the groom “de la Rep. Oriental, de veinte y cuatro”. The wife matches, the parents match, and twenty-four in February 1911 fits a birth on 29 October 1886 at Montevideo exactly. They are one man, and the two rows are now one. The index had him born 1887 at Almagro, Buenos Aires; the act says Oriental, and the indexer had defaulted the birthplace to the place of the wedding.

Documented That act also spells out a letter. On the two Montevideo acts he is only ever Alejandro M. Lerena, and on the enrolment cards of Cajón 985 he is only ever Alejandro M. Lerena. In the priest’s hand at San Carlos in 1911 the initial is written out: Máximo. So the expansion of that M. is now on a plate rather than in a tree, and the Alejandro M. Lerena of Cajón 985 carries the same one. It is worth being exact about what that does and does not do: it joins the cards to the Montevideo acts, which were never in doubt — the same wife and the same children stand on both. It does not join either of them to Avelino’s son.

Inferred Which makes the family’s road visible. Héctor born Montevideo 1886, Emilia Alberta Montevideo 1888, Roberto Juan in Misiones 1891, Rómer Rosario in Buenos Aires 1895, then Elisa Cornelia 1898, Carmen Romualda 1901 and María Luisa 1903 — the last three baptised together late, in 1905, 1907 and 1908. They left Montevideo between 1888 and 1891 and crossed the country before settling in the capital. Julia is italiana in 1886 and austriaca in 1888, aged 22 and then 24 — both written in front of her, and this archive records both rather than picking.
