The Cape, the Rand, and a founder cluster

South Africa

Sixty-one Lerenas in a country far from Iberia and the Americas, out of two thousand in the world. That is not scatter. That is a family.

Two South African hits that are not ours, written down so nobody excludes them twice

A substring search for this surname in South African indexes throws up two things that look like early Lerenas and are not. Both were run down and both are dead ends, and an exclusion that is not written down gets made again.

Documented A 1928 criminal case names a “Lerenani Matchubani”. Lerenani is a Tswana given name — not this surname, and not a surname at all.

Documented A notarial protocol has “Johannes Smook transferring the slave Jek of Ceylon to Goris Janse Lerenaar” (ZA KAB NCD 1/37, 112). Lerenaar is a different surname — a Dutch Cape one — and the document is an eighteenth-century slave transfer that predates any Lerena arrival in South Africa by a century.

They belong with the 1921 East London marriage where Lerena turns out to be the bride’s given name, already excluded in the register. All three are index noise from substring matching, and all three cost an hour each to dismiss the first time.

The founder cluster

Documented Forebears records 61 bearers of Lerena in South Africa, an incidence of 1 in 888,159. Against a worldwide total of only 2,124, South Africa holds close to three per cent of every Lerena alive — a country with no Spanish colonial past and no River Plate migration stream to speak of.

A rare surname appearing in a country it has no general reason to be in, at that concentration, has one ordinary explanation: one family arrived and multiplied. That is exactly what the family testimony describes. The distribution and the story corroborate each other, and neither was derived from the other.

Stated carefully: this makes the South African Lerenas likely to include one large kindred. It does not prove that they are all one — see the correction below.

Corrected, 8 September 2026

This page originally read the 61 as, in effect, a single family. That was too strong. A deeds register of 1927–47 records the deceased estate of Lucas Lerena, whose widow was Rachel Lerenia, born Maleka — Maleka being a Sotho or Tswana surname — at B. Eaton Township. A Pretoria church membership roll of 1939 carries a Hanson Seko Lerena.

So there are at least two unconnected Lerena populations in South Africa: this Argentine-descended family of the Cape and the Rand, and one or more Black South African families bearing the same or a near-identical name. Nothing links them, and this archive asserts no link.

The founder-cluster reading still holds for this family — the descent from Pablo Armando Lerena is documented, and the racing dynasty descending from him is public record. But the raw figure of 61 cannot be treated as one kindred, and the arithmetic that once did so is withdrawn.

The family's own footing here

Documented — from the Defranceski archive David Defranceski's MyHeritage tree is titled My Family Tree (Defranceski, Lerena, Taylor, Barry, Blazevic, Booyzen). The surnames in that title are themselves evidence:

  • Booyzen — an Afrikaans name (also written Booysen, Booyzen, Booyse). Its presence means the Lerena line married into an established Afrikaner family, and that the South African footing was not a brief posting but a settlement.
  • Taylor and Barry — English-language names, consistent with the Cape and the Rand. Barry is worth a second look: the Barrys were a substantial nineteenth-century merchant house in the Swellendam and Overberg districts of the Cape, in the same world as the Karoo ostrich trade. Suggestive only — a common surname, and no link established

Documented The Defranceski family map records Johannesburg as the place where David was born and where his father Ivan settled, and then "David Ivan and Cheryl move to Brisbane". The Lerena line therefore reaches Australia through Johannesburg, not directly from the Americas.

The ostrich farm

Family testimony The great-grandfather is believed to have kept an ostrich farm.

This is a more precise claim than it sounds, because ostrich farming in South Africa was concentrated in place and time. The industry centred on Oudtshoorn and the Little Karoo, and the feather boom ran from the 1880s to its collapse in 1914, when fashion and the war ended the trade almost overnight. Fortunes were made and lost inside a single generation, and the "feather palaces" of Oudtshoorn still stand.

So the claim carries a date and a district with it. If it is true, it places the family in the Cape — plausibly the Oudtshoorn district — and most likely before 1914. That is a testable proposition, and a narrow one.

Now doubtful

Everything since found points away from it. He was a Cape Town racing man — stables at Rugby, a training yard taking apprentices, a Newlands address, and "Race Horse Owner" on his death certificate — not a Karoo farmer. A search of South African deeds registers returns no land of his at all: the only Lerena property found is two suburban plots of 613 and 1,353 square metres belonging to his son Nuno Fernando in the 1950s. House erven, not a farm.

That is not disproof. Deeds coverage is partial, an ostrich farm could have been leased rather than owned, and Oudtshoorn's records have still not been searched.

And the family says he did hold land

"He owned a lot of land up around the Blouberg area. We do not know what he did." Blouberg is the coast north of Cape Town, beyond Milnerton and Rugby — where his stables were. If that is right, then the deeds sweep failed to find land that exists, and the likeliest reason is the search rather than the claim: his surname is written Lerena, Lerina and Larena across different records. The registers should be worked again, under every spelling, for Blouberg and Milnerton. Hypotheses →

An ostrich farmer is also among the occupations the family records for him — "unconfirmed, advised by Sue Taylor". So the tradition has a named source, which is more than it had this morning. Open questions →

The Government Gazette, read in full

On 15 September 2026 this archive re-sourced its South African dates from gazettes.africa — free, official, and fuller than the commercial index it replaces. A Form J193 estate notice prints the estate number, the forenames, the date of birth, the identity number, the address, the date of death, the surviving spouse and the executor. The index gave years; the gazette gives days.

The three sons of the 1950 estate. “19962/95 — Lerena, Ricardo Juan Carlos, 6 January 1920, 2001065053080, 6 Barbeque Agricultural Holdings, Randburg, 21 December 1994; Doreen May Lerena.” The identity number confirms the birth date out of its own first six digits. “6746/71 — LERENA Nuno Fernando born on 6 October 1924… of 24 Leiden Road, Gerdview, Germiston, who died on 1 July 1971, and surviving spouse Catherine Mary Sophia Lerena, born 25 March 1929.” And “1518/73 — LERENA, Rogue Luis Armando, 6/12/05, 331/402089, 20 Park Lane, Florida, 29/1/73.”

Two of those change the shape of the family. The 1950 liquidation account made Nuno Fernando’s capital payable when he “attains the age of thirty years — i.e. 6 October 1954”, which implies a birth on 6 October 1924: two unrelated documents, twenty-one years apart, agreeing to the day. And Rogue Luis Armando was born in 1905, which makes him the eldest of the three by fifteen years, and forty-four when his father died — raising a question this archive had not asked, since Pablo Armando was born at Rosario in 1882 and the eight years between Northam and the Cape are still unaccounted for. A son born in 1905 may not have been born in South Africa at all.

Anton Armando Lerena’s death is no longer a tree’s word. “15127/2013 — Lerena, Anton Armando, 05-05-1952, 5206055175085, 158 London Lane, Knoppieslaagte, Centurion, 3 April 2003.” His estate was advertised ten years after he died, which is how this archive came to file him under 2013 and briefly believe two records described two men. One field is internally inconsistent and is not smoothed here: the typed birth date reads 05-05-1952 while the identity number encodes 1952-06-05, and every other entry on that page agrees with its own number. The year is certain and the day is not.

And the man himself is described by the state. “1847/50/754 — Robert Paul Lerena, racehorse trainer, of Newlands, Cape.” This archive had “Trainer” from his own 1940 passport; the gazette is an independent statement made after his death, and it is more specific. A notice of 1986 lists P. R. Lerena Racing Stables, P.O. Box 425, Randburg — the same trade, thirty-five years later, in the town where Ricardo Juan Carlos lived and died.

Where the records are

NAAIRS — the National Archives

South Africa's National Automated Archival Information Retrieval System indexes estate files, deceased estates, wills and departmental records across the national and provincial repositories. A surname this rare should be searchable outright. Deceased estate files are the highest-value target: they name the widow, the children, the farm and the assets.

Civil registration

Births, marriages and deaths held by the Department of Home Affairs, with older registers in the archives. Cheryl's father's and grandfather's records are the direct route up the line.

Government gazettes

For the exequatur recognising a foreign consul — the crispest proof of the consular claim, and dated. Union of South Africa gazettes from 1910; Cape Colony gazettes before that.

Newspapers

The Cape and Rand press reported consular appointments, race meetings and farm sales alike. A single social column could carry the name, the post and the farm in one paragraph.

Oudtshoorn and the feather trade

The C.P. Nel Museum at Oudtshoorn and the Cape archives hold the records of the feather boom — farm registers, buyers, auction records. If there was a Lerena ostrich farm, this is where it is written down.

The Jockey Club and the stud book

Registrations of owners, breeders and imported bloodstock. See The horses.

The emigration to Australia

The last leg — Johannesburg to Brisbane — is the best-documented part of the whole line and the easiest to close, because it is recent and Australian records are good. It is deliberately left until the living generations can be handled properly: living people are omitted from this build, and the Australian chapter mostly concerns them. Method →

The only grave

13 September 2026. The first physical trace of this family found anywhere.

A grave kerb at Maitland, Woltemade Cemetery — poured concrete edging, not a headstone — with one word cut into its face in inset lettering:

LERENA

and nothing else — no forename, no dates, no epitaph. Above the kerb, bare earth and grass.

Maitland Woltemade is where both of them are buried. Mary Septima in November 1935, grave 7719A, Roman Catholic, Cemetery no. 1, from the interment register; and Pablo Armando on 19 March 1950 — “intended place of burial: Maitland Cemetery no 1” on his death certificate.

And it is the only Lerena grave in the entire eGGSA Western Cape collection. The variants were checked the same afternoon: LERIN — which would catch Lerina and Lerini — returns nothing; Lerena as a substring also covers Llerena, with no separate hit; LAREN returns thirty-nine records, every one of them Clarence or its like.

It cannot be assigned to a person. A surname on a kerb, with no dates and no forename, is equally consistent with her grave, with his, or with a family plot holding both — and this archive does not turn consistent with into is. What would settle it is the grave number: hers is recorded as 7719A, and the municipal cemetery office holds the Woltemade register.

One small thing it does say. The stone reads LERENA — which runs, lightly, against the hypothesis raised from the DNA that the name might be nearer Lerina, the spelling on his death certificate. Not decisive: a kerb may postdate the burial, and a mason cuts what he is given. But it is evidence, and it points the other way. The DNA →

The five deaths of 1999, tested

A commercial index carried five members of this family with the year 1999, among them a married couple and two children aged thirteen and eight. Either something terrible happened, or the index has a default-year artefact, and a summary row cannot tell you which.

The free Government Gazette was used to test it. The artefact reading is dead for three of the five. But the absences prove nothing, and the row that decides how the whole table reads is the control at the top of it.

Three records found by running a control

The target was Paul Raymond Lerena's 2001 estate notice. It was not reached. But the control query run first — the one that proves the search works before any zero is believed — returned forty-two documents, and three of them this archive did not hold.

All three are snippet only. The search index is open; the document viewer sits behind a bot challenge that was not worked around, so nothing below has been read on the page itself. And gazette OCR reads across the gutter of a two-column notice, so names that appear adjacent in a snippet are not necessarily adjacent on the page.

The cemetery register answers it: catastrophe, not artefact

A commercial index carried five members of this family with the year 1999, and this archive refused to say which it was — either something terrible happened, or the index has a default-year artefact, and a summary row cannot tell you which. The Government Gazette narrowed it but could not reach the wife or the children.

The Johannesburg cemetery register reaches all of them, and it is free. Pierre Frederick and Richard both died on 12 November 1999; Gayle and Jeffrey were buried with them on 22 November; and all four share a burial registration of 18 November 1999. Their four record identifiers are consecutive — four sequential entries, one family, entered in a single act of registration.

It also does something row 17 has been waiting for: Doreen May Lerena was the last person here whose dates rested on the commercial index, and she is now free-sourced with a day — died 17 November 1999, five days after the others.

The other archives

The Lerena Archive is one of seven, each following a different line of the same family. They use the same method and the same five words for how sure a thing is.

  • The Defranceschi Archive Istria · Kvarner · Carnia A family written down under eight sovereigns, in four languages, and spelled at least nine different ways. Defranceschi · De Franceschi · Defranceski · Defrančeski
  • The Falco Archive Arienzo, near Naples Nine documented generations born, married and buried in one valley, and then a migrant ship to Brisbane. Falco · Zampiello · Morgillo · Crisci · Liguori · Maione · Rivetti · Annecchino
  • The Blažević Archive Senj and the Krmpotska mountains An evidence-first archive for the family of Hedviga Blažević of Senj — carriers, a tobacco factory, and a wall grave. Blažević · Papić · Vukelić · Žubrinić · Kalanj
  • The Booyzen Archive Graaff-Reinet · Cradock · Johannesburg A Cape Dutch family that kept marrying British women, changed one letter of its name in a war, and came off the train at Johannesburg with nothing. Booyzen · Booysen · Kolbe · Barry · Downing · van Jaarsveld · Schoeman
  • The D'Arcy Archive England and Ireland · Parramatta · Brisbane Out with the 39th Regiment in 1825, and four generations afterwards in two Brisbane cemeteries. D'Arcy · Saniger · Sneyd · Hornby · Wakefield · Atkinson · Murdoch
  • The Mazza Archive Sicily and Calabria · Brisbane Four Italian quarters — Mazza of Piedimonte Etneo, and Prostamo, Arena and Polistena of Calabria — ending in four graves in a row at Nudgee. Mazza · Prostamo · Arena · Polistena
