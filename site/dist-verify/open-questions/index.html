Published so strangers can help

Open Questions

Published deliberately. An archive that lists only its answers is advertising; one that lists its questions can be helped by strangers.

1. Who were Juan Carlos and María Lerena?

Answered on 10 September 2026, and immediately replaced by a harder question. The army enrolment card names Pablo Armando’s parents: Juan Carlos Lerena and María Lerena. Nothing else is known of either of them — not a date, not a marriage, not a death.

  • María’s maiden name — and one branch of it now closed. She is written with her husband’s surname. On these cards hijo de X y de Y usually gives each parent’s own name — which would make her a Lerena by birth and the marriage one between kin — but some clerks used the married name. The card cannot tell.

    A marriage between kin needs a dispensation, so on 13 September the Arquidiócesis de Montevideo dispensation register, 1856–1932, was searched by its own formulae. It holds eight Lerena dispensations in seventy-six years — and no Juan Carlos, and no Lerena–Lerena pairing at all. Every Lerena in it marries out: Fernández, Peláez, Álvarez, Cordero, Márquez.

    So if she was a Lerena by birth, they did not marry in the archdiocese of Montevideo. What that does not show is that they did not marry there at all — the register records only exceptions, and an ordinary marriage between unrelated people leaves no trace in it. A weak negative, filed as one.
  • The rest of Cajón 985. The cards are filed alphabetically, so every Argentine of enrolment age surnamed Lerena sits within a few frames of this one, each naming his own parents. Brothers, cousins and the shape of the family are sitting unread in that drawer.
  • The missing baptism, and it is now much starker. As of 13 September both volumes of the Rosario baptism index have been read end to end — 1731 to 1901 — and the surname appears once in a hundred and seventy years. 1881, 1882 and 1883 were also swept across all twenty-six letter sections for a Pablo Armando under any surname. He is not there; neither is his family, before him or after him. That is no longer a gap in the searching. What has been searched →
  • And the one Lerena who is there was Uruguayan. Folio 229, February 1866: the act says of both parents “naturales del E. Oriental”. If the surname’s home in the River Plate is Montevideo, then Juan Carlos Lerena came to Rosario from somewhere, and the first somewhere to try is across the river. Does anyone hold a Uruguayan Lerena line with a Juan Carlos born in the 1840s or 1850s, married to a María? That is the single most useful thing a stranger could tell this archive today. Hypothesis 1c →
  • The consular matrícula. A man enrolling at the Cape Town consulate in 1908 had a file there. The request to the Argentine foreign ministry is already lodged.
  • And the confirmations are now read, and he is not in them. The Rosario confirmation register — every entry naming both parents — has been read page by page from 15 May 1885 to the 1897 section: about two and a half thousand children, and no Lerena under any of seven spellings. He was three when it opens and fifteen when it closes, and children appear at every age from two to fifteen. Two separate Rosario registers have now been swept end to end, and neither holds him. How it was read →

14 September 2026 — the sharpest test there is, and it has now been run

The card names both parents Lerena. Either María married into the name, or she was born to it — and Spanish naming makes that testable in a single query. A child of two Lerenas carries both surnames: Lerena Lerena. In two weeks nobody had searched for the compound.

Run against the Uruguayan Catholic registers it returned four documents; run against the whole transcribed corpus, without a country filter, forty-three. Every one was opened and every transcript read.

All forty-three are transcription doublings. “Pío Lerena . Lerena” across a line end; “Zelmira Lerena, Lerena argentino”; “Celidonio B. de Lerena Lerena”; “G. Lerena Lerena” written across a printed title page at Chacabuco. The machine repeats a word at a line or page break, and it produced the same false positive four times over before the pattern was obvious. The only genuine compounds among the forty-three are Colombian — an Elena Lerena Llerena at Calamar in the 1990s, a different family on a different continent.

So no child of two Lerenas is recorded under the compound anywhere FamilySearch has transcribed. That is a controlled negative, not a silent one: the query demonstrably ran.

Documented 20 September 2026 — one of them has now been read off the parish image, and it holds. A sweep of Uruguayan deaths made for an entirely different reason returned “Carmen Lerena Lerena”, buried at the Cementerio del Cordón, Montevideo. The forty-three above were each checked against a transcript; this one was checked against the act. Film 007713392, image 2511: the margin reads simply “Carmen Lerena.” in the clerk’s own hand, and the body “de edad de tres añ[os], hija legítima de Justo Lerena y de Juana Quinta[na]”. One Lerena parent. The compound exists only in the machine.

The index is also a year out — it gives 1840, the act reads “Mil ochocientos cuarenta y uno”, and the burial below it says the same. And the act leaves behind a household this archive did not have: Justo Lerena and Juana Quintana of Montevideo, and their daughter Carmen, buried at three. No tie to Sancho is asserted; he had a daughter Justa, and an echo of a forename is not an argument. All three in the register →

What it does not do is close the question. A woman can be a Lerena by birth without her children ever being written so — the card itself writes Pablo Armando simply Lerena — and full text reaches only what has been transcribed, which at Rosario de Santa Fe is thin. It lowers the probability. It does not settle it. And it means no Lerena–Lerena marriage should now be asserted anywhere in this archive.

The same search turned up the first documented María Lerena of roughly the right generation — in an 1911 Montevideo dispensation that draws its own family tree. She married Eliseo Cardozo, where Pablo Armando’s mother stands beside Juan Carlos Lerena, so she is almost certainly not the one. She is recorded anyway, and recorded as ruled out, because this archive has twice built on a María it had not pinned down. In the register →

2. Was he really a Consul General?

  • Search the Government Gazette for the exequatur — the host state's formal recognition of a foreign consul. Crisp, dated, decisive.
  • Search the Argentine Boletín Oficial and the Foreign Ministry's diplomatic lists for the appointment.
  • Test the honorary-consul hypothesis. A resident merchant holding an honorary post would appear in the gazettes but not in the career foreign-service lists — and would reconcile a diplomat with a man who bred horses and farmed ostriches. Hypothesis
  • Establish which post: Cape Town, Johannesburg, Pretoria or Durban, and in which years.

3. The horses

  • Search the South African Stud Book and Jockey Club registers for Lerena as owner, breeder or importer — particularly imports from Argentina, which would corroborate the claim directly.
  • Search the Argentine Stud Book at the other end.
  • Establish who Gilberto Justiniano Lerena Lenguas was in family terms — his dates, household and descendants — and whether he connects to this line at all. The horses →
  • Trace Haras El Viejo: its records, its sale or dispersal, and its succession.

4. The ostrich farm

  • Search the Oudtshoorn and Little Karoo farm records, and the C.P. Nel Museum's holdings on the feather trade.
  • Test the date. The feather boom collapsed in 1914; if the farm was real it most likely predates that, which in turn constrains when the family arrived.
  • Search the deeds registry for land held under the surname.

5. Basque or Extremaduran?

  • Obtain the INE provincial distribution for Spain. Forebears summarises the Spanish Lerenas as chiefly La Rioja, Andalusia and Madrid — awkward for both theories, and it needs the real figures rather than a summary.
  • Look for Lerena and Llerena alternating for the same individual in River Plate records. That, more than any etymology, settles whether the two names are one.
  • Find the emigration record, which normally names the town of origin outright. The name →

6. Rosario, and the crossing

  • Search Santa Fe civil registration and the Rosario parish registers for the family.
  • Search CEMLA and the Buenos Aires passenger lists for the arrival from Europe.
  • Find the outbound crossing to South Africa — the date, the ship, and who travelled.
  • Keep Uruguay live throughout. It has the world's densest Lerena population, and if Argentina stalls it is the next move, not the last resort. Argentina →

7. The South African kindred

  • Establish whether the 61 South African Lerenas are indeed one family, and whether this line founded it. South Africa →
  • Work out the Booyzen marriage — an Afrikaner connection, and evidence of settlement rather than a posting.
  • Test whether Taylor and Barry attach to this line or to another branch of the tree. Unexamined

The other archives

The Lerena Archive is one of seven, each following a different line of the same family. They use the same method and the same five words for how sure a thing is.

  • The Defranceschi Archive Istria · Kvarner · Carnia A family written down under eight sovereigns, in four languages, and spelled at least nine different ways. Defranceschi · De Franceschi · Defranceski · Defrančeski
  • The Falco Archive Arienzo, near Naples Nine documented generations born, married and buried in one valley, and then a migrant ship to Brisbane. Falco · Zampiello · Morgillo · Crisci · Liguori · Maione · Rivetti · Annecchino
  • The Blažević Archive Senj and the Krmpotska mountains An evidence-first archive for the family of Hedviga Blažević of Senj — carriers, a tobacco factory, and a wall grave. Blažević · Papić · Vukelić · Žubrinić · Kalanj
  • The Booyzen Archive Graaff-Reinet · Cradock · Johannesburg A Cape Dutch family that kept marrying British women, changed one letter of its name in a war, and came off the train at Johannesburg with nothing. Booyzen · Booysen · Kolbe · Barry · Downing · van Jaarsveld · Schoeman
  • The D'Arcy Archive England and Ireland · Parramatta · Brisbane Out with the 39th Regiment in 1825, and four generations afterwards in two Brisbane cemeteries. D'Arcy · Saniger · Sneyd · Hornby · Wakefield · Atkinson · Murdoch
  • The Mazza Archive Sicily and Calabria · Brisbane Four Italian quarters — Mazza of Piedimonte Etneo, and Prostamo, Arena and Polistena of Calabria — ending in four graves in a row at Nudgee. Mazza · Prostamo · Arena · Polistena

And for children: Our Family — The same ten families, told for a six-year-old.

An evidence-first family archive for the Lerena family — of Rosario and the River Plate, of the Cape and the Rand, and of Brisbane. Every claim carries its confidence. Living people are named — name and relationship only, never a date, a place, a photograph or a record.
