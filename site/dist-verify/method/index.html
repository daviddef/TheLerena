How this archive decides what it will say

Method

The same method as the Falco and Defranceski archives. Every claim is tiered, and the tier is shown. An archive that cannot say what it does not know is not an archive.

The five words

All seven of these family archives grade a claim with the same five words, so a level means the same thing wherever you meet it. The word is always printed next to the colour, because a reader should never have to know the palette to know the confidence.

Documented
A record was read, and it says this. The source is named.
Probable
The evidence points one way and nothing found so far points the other. Not proven.
Inferred
Reasoned from what is known — a gap of the right size, a name in the right place — rather than witnessed in a record.
Family
Told, remembered, or written on a family chart. Kept, because discarding it would lose the lead; labelled, because it is not evidence.
Disputed
Two sources disagree and neither has been ruled out. Both are shown.
Not claimed

Some pages carry this instead. It states the thing a reader is likely to assume — the connection that would be tidy, the identification that almost works — and says plainly that this archive does not assert it. An archive is judged as much by what it refuses to say as by what it says.

The tiers

Documented A named source with a reference, and where possible the scan itself.
Inferred A reasoned conclusion from documented facts, with the reasoning written out so that it can be overturned by anyone who finds it wanting.
Superseded Asserted in the family record, and now displaced by a document that says otherwise. The old claim is kept visible, not deleted.
Disputed Asserted in the family record but unsupported, or contradicted, by what can be seen.
Family lore Told, remembered, not corroborated. Kept because it is precious; labelled because pretending otherwise is how family myths become family history.

This archive starts at the bottom of the ladder

Nearly everything specific to this family is presently family lore. The Argentine origin, Rosario, the consulship, the horses, the ostriches — all of it is testimony, and it is presented as testimony. What is documented so far is largely context: the rarity and distribution of the surname, the town of Llerena, the Argentine Stud Book, the Oudtshoorn feather boom.

Context is not evidence about a family. It is the backdrop against which that family has to be placed, and it is worth having early because it tells you which claims are likely, which are hard, and where to look. It is never allowed to stand in for a record.

Rarity is evidence, and it has limits

With 2,124 bearers worldwide, Lerena is rare enough that clustering carries real information — the 61 in South Africa are very unlikely to be unrelated strangers. The archive will therefore let distribution propose connections.

It will not let distribution prove them. A proposal built on rarity is written down as inferred, with its reasoning exposed, and it stays inferred until a register says otherwise.

Living people

Living people are omitted from the build entirely — not hidden, not gated, not present in the output. Because this line reaches the present within three generations, that removes a substantial part of the story from public view, including most of the South African and all of the Australian chapter. The material is kept in the private working files; it is simply not published.

Public figures are treated differently, and only as public figures: a jockey's race record or a breeder's stud book entry is published information and may be cited as such. No claim of relationship to a living person is made from a shared surname.

A removal request is honoured within days, without argument and without requiring a reason.

A null is not a finding until a control says so

Most of what this archive has established is negative — that Pablo Armando is not in the Rosario baptisms, that Juan Carlos Lerena is in no record anywhere. Negative findings are only as good as the search that produced them, and a search can fail in a way that looks exactly like an answer.

On 14 September 2026 this archive nearly recorded three false nulls in ten minutes. FamilySearch’s results page renders the string “Historical Record Search Results (0)” while the query is still running — a complete, composed, confident page that cannot be told apart from a genuinely empty result.

It was caught only by running a control. The same query shape was aimed at Sancho Lerena, a man for whom five records had been pulled twenty minutes earlier. It returned zero too. Since that answer was known to be false, the fault had to be in the query rather than in the name. Waiting longer returned the five records — and then 210, and then 822.

This is the same class of failure as the zero-length response body already logged against this source: the service failing in a way that resembles success. It is worse than an outage, because an outage announces itself.

So the rule: a null is not written down until a control query, in the same form, in the same session, returns a result already known to exist. The archive learned this once before and more expensively, over the 1895 census, where it read an enumerator’s abbreviation as a husband’s surname with no control case and had to retract within the hour. The second time the lesson arrived it cost nothing.

15 September 2026 — the rule decided four questions in one day, and only one of them the way it was expected to

It saved a day of work once. This archive had planned to read three hundred images of a regiment’s chaplaincy book by hand, looking for Sancho Llerena. Before starting, one control — a search for the chaplain’s own name, Berroeta — showed the book was in the full-text index. His absence from it was therefore already evidence, and the three hundred images were never read.

It voided a null that looked clean. Searching Spanish records for Félix Llerena and Rosalía Aguilar returned zero. The control — asking what Andalusian collections exist at all — showed the full-text corpus for Andalusia holds nobility and notarial records, not parish registers. The zero meant not findable by this instrument, not not there.

It voided two more, in the same hour. Two exact-surname searches returned zero with the “No Results Found” line present — normally this archive’s proof that a search ran. The control showed the same query shape could not retrieve a record known to be in the collection, because the indexed surname was a compound (Lerena y Armenta) that exact matching will not reach. A null from a query that cannot find what is there is not a null.

And once it confirmed a negative worth keeping. Sancho’s burial is in no index. The control — a common surname, same city, same decade — returned church burials from his own family’s parish dated 1832, 1833 and 1838. So the records exist, are indexed, and do not contain him. That is the only one of the four that survived, and it is the only one this archive would publish.

21 September 2026 — the control passed, and the null was false anyway

The rule above says a null is not written down until a control in the same form and the same session returns a known result. On 21 September that rule was satisfied and the answer was still a lie.

A parent-pair query on Avelino Lerena and Celedonia Villademoros returned a clean zero. The control — the identical query shape on Alberto Lerena and María Inés Larriera, minutes earlier — had returned sixteen records. Deleting the mother’s surname and keeping only “Celedonia” turned the zero into seven, two of which are christenings already published on this site.

So a control licenses the shape of a query and says nothing about the value you put in it. Larriera has one spelling in this register. Villademoros has four — Villademoros, Villardemoros, Villademoro, and the compound the children carry. The control could not fail, because it was asked about the one surname that could not go wrong. What caught the zero was not method at all: it was that the archive already held two of the records it was hiding.

Which sharpens the older rule rather than replacing it. A nil from a derived index is, in the first instance, a fact about the index. It becomes a fact about the family only when the index’s coverage of the relevant registers has been established — and coverage is not one thing. It can fail at the collection, as it did for Andalusian parish registers; at the query shape, as it did for the compound Lerena y Armenta; or, as here, at a single field the source failed to parse. Three different failures, one identical-looking zero.

This archive publishes negatives as evidence, because most of what it has established is negative. That is exactly why the bar has to keep rising. A published null needs a date, a control, and a statement of what the instrument could have reached.

Every disguise a false zero has worn

The rule above was not arrived at once. It was arrived at twelve times, and this is the catalogue — because a search that fails in a way that looks like an answer is the most dangerous thing this archive meets, and the next one will not look like any of these either.

Nine of these were written in working logs where no reader could see them — found on 20 September 2026 by asking which findings in this archive’s 136 working files reach no page at all. The two dated that day were caught by the habit rather than by luck, which is the only argument for keeping a habit.

On 15 September the working log recorded that the same trap had been seen four times in a month, in four different disguises. It is now twelve.

What they have in common is not the mechanism. A quota, a logout, a throttle, a block, a mislabelled reel and a counter that ignores its own parameters have nothing to do with one another. What they share is that each produced a page a careful person would have believed, and that in every single case the thing that caught it was a control query whose answer was already known.

And the mirror — a false positive that would have been believed

A full-text search for the exact phrase “Sancho Llerena” returns four pages. Page one is this family. Pages two to four are Spanish nobility proofs, notarial books and military records — and not one of them contains a man of that name.

One was opened to find out why. A 1599 proof of nobility for the Order of Alcántara reads, in transcript: “…es familiar del SANCHO, OFICIO LLERENA”. It is Santo Oficio … Llerena — the Inquisition tribunal seated at Llerena in Badajoz. The machine reads Santo Oficio as Sancho, oficio, and the phrase search then matches Sancho … Llerena across the two words.

The Inquisition sat at Llerena for three centuries, so those words stand next to each other in thousands of Spanish documents. Any search for this family in Spain will drown in them — and this archive was one step from recording that Sancho is an attested forename in Spanish Llerena families. It is not. That false fact would then have been used, quietly, to argue against Francisco.

A false zero is caught by a control. A false positive is caught only by opening the document — which is the other half of the same rule, and the more expensive half, because a hit that agrees with you asks to be believed.

The afternoon the search returned nothing, and said so politely

It passed every guard this archive had. The only thing that caught it was a control whose answer cannot be zero.

On the afternoon of 21 September 2026, a query returned “Historical Record Search Results (0)” with “No Results Found” printed beneath it.

That line is the thing this archive has been treating as proof of a real null — the positive assertion of emptiness, as opposed to a heading rendered before the data arrives. The count was stable across two reads twelve seconds apart. It was still zero after a ninety-second wait. And a control on the same query shape had passed earlier the same day.

All of it was wrong. The search was returning zero for everything.

What it actually was

One browser context had lost its authentication — and only one. The two FamilySearch searches handle that completely differently: asked the same question in the same minute, the full-text search redirected to the sign-in page — honest, unmistakable, one navigation — while the record search answered “0 — No Results Found”. The DeepZoom image host went on serving plates throughout, which is why a whole day of reading images had given no warning.

So there is a free test, and it costs one navigation: ask the full-text search anything at all. It admits a lost session. The record search hides one behind a null that satisfies every other rule on this page.

And this archive then got the remedy wrong, which is the part worth publishing. It concluded that the account was signed out and said so here, and on its work list, and that David would have to sign in again. The account was never signed out. A sibling archive on the same machine ran both searches in those same minutes and got 3,652 and 3,967, with the account name in the navigation bar. Each session’s in-app browser is a separate browser with its own cookies; one lost its auth and recovered by itself. In a fresh tab the González control returned 479,662 and the query that had returned ten that morning returned ten again. Before asking a person to fix something, open a second tab.

All three nulls held over for re-testing — Luis with Carolina, Carlos Ambrosio with Luisa, Juan Arturo with Isidora — were re-run against a live control and all three are still zero. Nothing was lost but the afternoon.

And the next day, the same guard caught a different fault

A parent-pair query on Sancho with María in Uruguay returned 0 — read twice, with the González control returning 479,660 minutes earlier. This time the service was healthy. The query shape was broken.

  • father’s given name Sancho, alone — 10,315
  • mother’s given name María, alone — 1,228,708
  • both together — 0
  • both, plus the two surnames — 6, every one of them real

Two fields matching five and seven figures cannot intersect at nothing in a country where María is the commonest name a mother has. A parent-pair query with given names only returns zero whatever the truth is, and says nothing about it. At least one parent surname must be supplied before a null from this shape means anything.

Arithmetic caught it again, and it is the only guard on this page that has never failed. Notice what it is doing: not asking whether the answer is plausible, but whether the answer is possible.

Three things the other archives on this machine sent back, each better than what this one had

  • A negative must carry proof that the instrument reached the place. Not “the search returned nothing” but here is the entry before where it would be, and here is the entry after, with the numbering continuous across the page break. That is a null carrying its own evidence. A control query only borrows evidence from something run nearby. (Luwinski.)
  • A positive proves the service was answering for that collection. Where access is per-collection rather than all-or-nothing, a hit in an open collection vouches for nothing in a restricted one. (Defranceski.)
  • A service that is honest in one place and fabricates in another, at the same moment, is worse than one that always lies. A consistent liar is caught once and distrusted for ever. This one redirected honestly in one browser and manufactured a null in another, in the same minute, on the same machine. (Booyzen.)

And the thing that hid it longest, which is worth more than any of the rules: the plate host went on serving images perfectly all afternoon, in the same browser, while the search fabricated. A day of successful image reads is not evidence that the search is healthy. Different hosts, different authentication, independent failure — and the one you are watching is not the one that is lying.

What proved it

Two facts, neither of them a matter of judgement:

  • Surname González, in Uruguay, returned 0. González is among the commonest surnames in the Spanish-speaking world; the Uruguayan civil registration runs to millions of records. That answer cannot be zero.
  • The same URL that had returned ten records that morning returned 0 that afternoon — Avelino Lerena with Ambrosia Traibel, the ten acts this archive had spent the day reading.

What it nearly cost

The zeros looked like a discovery. A man whose baptism this archive has read off the plate — Alberto Octavio Lerena, act 585 of 7 October 1850 — returned nothing when searched by name. The obvious reading was a real and interesting mechanism: that the parent-pair query reaches only the keyed index, and cannot see acts that exist in the full-text corpus alone.

That is a good theory, it explains the evidence, and it is false. It was about to be written up as a finding, with consequences for every null in this archive drawn from a parent-pair query. It was built entirely on an outage.

So the rule gains two words. A control must be recent — the service was healthy in the morning and lying by the afternoon — and its answer must be arithmetically impossible to be zero. A control that merely ought to return something is a control that fails silently alongside the query it was meant to guard.

One class of zero survives untouched, and it is worth naming: a zero that was turned into a result by deleting a parameter. Villademoros 0→7 and Hermenegilda 0→3 both stand, because a positive result proves the service was answering at that moment. Only the zeros that stayed zero are in doubt.

How much of this register has a machine read, and nobody checked

Two failures on one day, from the same cause, pointing in opposite directions.

This archive’s standing rule is that an index is not evidence and the image must be opened. On 21 September 2026 it found out how often it had kept that rule.

Sixty-one register rows cited “full-text transcript read” — which means a machine read it — and named no image. That is more than half the rows carrying a source at all. A tool now ranks the unopened plates by how many people depend on each one, because one image can settle seven rows at once.

What the machine got wrong

The 1892 marriage act of Alfredo Justiniano Lerena Traibel gave his bride as Hermenegilda Carolina Gavarro, daughter of Gerónimo Gavarro and Carolina Juárez. The plate says “hija legítima de Don Gerónimo Gavazzo y Doña Carolina Suárez”. Three surnames, wrong for a week, and confirmed wrong by a second image forty-eight years later. Gavazzo is a known Montevideo surname and Gavarro is not, which is the kind of tell that should have prompted the check.

What the machine got wrong in the other direction

The top-ranked unopened plate was the one carrying the archive’s most consequential proper noun: the 1831 baptism at the Cordón where the President of Uruguay stood godfather to Sancho’s grandson. Seven register rows rest on it. It was opened — film 007713388, image 00726 — and the act reads:

“padrinos Dn. Frutuso Ribera, Presidente de la República y su esposa Da. Bernardina Fragoso”

Frutuso Ribera, with a B. The transcript had printed Fructuoso Rivera — the spelling in every history book, and not what the page says. The finding holds and is stronger than it was, because the act states the man’s office and names his wife: in December 1831 the President of the Republic was Fructuoso Rivera and his wife was Bernardina Fragoso. Two independent facts on one line, either of which identifies him alone. The same page spells the grandfather Llerena, with the double L.

Both failures are one failure. A machine transcript is reliable about dates, ages, relationships and formulae, and unreliable about proper nouns — it invents a plausible name where it cannot read one, and it writes the famous spelling over the page’s own where it can. So the rule is not “distrust transcripts”. It is: a transcript is a finding-aid for everything except the names, and the names are usually why you went.

The sweep is finished — sixty-one to nothing

Every one of those rows has now been read off a plate. Twenty images, in one day. The tool reports 0 of 153, and what the sweep actually cost is the argument for having run it:

  • Four claims about this family’s spelling were the machine’s, not the scribe’s — Abelino Serena is LERENA; Sancho Lorena is LERENA; the LORENA of an 1889 act is its margin, not its sentence; and Fructuoso Rivera is the history books’ spelling written over the page’s own Frutuso Ribera.
  • Five surnames were simply wrong. Gavarro is Gavazzo, Juárez is Suárez, Florencia is Florinda, Seguera is Segueira, and “Del Suando” is Zalsamendi — a Basque name no search for the other could have reached.
  • One baptism was in the wrong parish and one citation pointed at the wrong film.
  • A birthplace was wrong in a way that changed the family’s shape: María Fernández is “natural de Asturias”, not of Montevideo.
  • And about twenty-five people who were standing on the acts had never been entered at all — among them Sancho’s parents-in-law, two grandparents drawn at the apex of a priest’s kinship diagram, a child of Justa Pastora, and Adolfo Lerena.

A zero here is a statement about today, not a property of the archive. Every new row that cites a transcript and names no image puts the count back up, which is the point of printing it: python3 tools/transcript-only.py.

A child born six years after his father died

The date on an index entry can be the date of the copy, not of the act. This one moved a man’s birth by forty-four years.

On 21 September 2026 a search for the children of Avelino Lerena and Ambrosia Traibel returned ten records. Three of them were dated 1896 and 1919. Avelino died in 1890. Ambrosia was born around 1820.

The tempting reading is that the index has attached the wrong parents, and that those three are other people’s children. The opposite was true. The parents are right and the dates are wrong, because the film is not a baptism register at all — it is a register of certified copies issued in 1896, and the indexer took the year off the certificate and the day and month off the act inside it.

The act, read off film 008166435, image 02850, opens: “Certifico: que en el libro 1.º de Bautismos … en el folio 135, se lee la partida siguiente … El veinte y uno de Noviembre de 1852”.

The tell is cheap and it is worth memorising. Two different children of one couple were indexed with the same birth date, 16 March 1896. The second tell is an impossible godparent: the man who stood at this font is Pedro Antonio Lerena, born 1793 — the child’s uncle, and a man who could not be in a room in 1896.

Why the book exists is written on every page of it. Each certificate opens “yo Luis Cincinato Bollo, Director General del Registro del Estado Civil, he certificado, de acuerdo con lo dispuesto por la Ley de 9 de Julio de 1888”. Uruguay took registration off the parishes in 1879 and then had the older baptisms copied across. So a family’s whole set of acts can sit in one 1896 volume, decades out of their own order — and the index will date every one of them to 1896. This is not a fault in one record. It is the shape of a collection.

And the index is not noise. It keeps the day and the month and replaces only the year, so it is two thirds of a date and the film supplies the third. The two brothers really were born on 16 March — in 1852 and 1857, five years to the day apart.

The one left untested was tested, and it is the same act twice

This section first went up naming a third record — a christening indexed 9 May 1919, born 13 August 1918 — as untested, because its godfather bore a name that was alive in this family in 1919.

The act was then read. It is dated 31 December 1865, the child is María Adelina Lerena, born 13 August 1865, and her godparents are “Dn. Ambrosio Lerena y Da. Carlota Muñoz” — the same two names the 1919 entry carries, and the same 13 August. It is one baptism, certified a second time on a different film, and dated by the index to that second certificate. One act, two films, two index entries, and fifty-three years between the two children they appear to describe.

And it is worth stating as a rule, because the two entries looked like corroboration. Two records, two collections, two films, two indexers, agreeing on a 13 August and on a set of godparents. Any reasonable reader would have called that independent confirmation. It is one act. Documents that descend from a single original are one witness, however many times they are copied, filed and indexed — and copying multiplies the entries while adding nothing to the evidence. Real confirmation needs a different occasion or a different mouth: a birth act against a marriage act, a parish register against a civil registration, a census against a passport.

This archive had already met the same shape from the other end and not named it. The three baptisms at La Mauricia in January 1846 all give the year in the identical words, “del pasado año cuarenta y tres” — including one for a different family. Three acts agreeing, written by one priest in one sitting, and the agreement means nothing: the children cannot all have been born that year. One hand, one occasion, one witness.

It also settled a contradiction this archive had flagged and could not resolve. Her 1946 death registration gives her birth as 1861; her brother Juan Arturo was born in February 1861, so no sister could be. The act says 1865, and the reasoning that doubted the registration was right for the right reason.

Before you list what to buy, list what you have

On 16 September 2026 this archive drew up a ranked list of files for a researcher going to the National Archives in Pretoria, where the first file costs R2,850. It put two Transvaal death notices first and second.

Both had been sitting on David’s own disk, photographed, since November 2021. The same folder held the 1911 marriage register, which was on the work list as a thing to go and find. Nobody had ever opened it.

The failure was not carelessness about sources. It was a gap in the bookkeeping. This archive kept a careful register of what it had read and no register at all of what it already held — and the two are not the same thing. A document can be in the house, scanned, for five years, and be invisible to an archive that only records its own searching.

So: before any list of documents to acquire, list the documents already held. The family’s own papers are a repository like any other, and they are the only one that charges nothing and is open on a Sunday.

What came out of that folder in twenty minutes is on The papers in the house: a marriage act, a religion, a trade nine years earlier than anything else held, a birthplace that closed the very question the trip was to answer, and a blank line on a consular form that says more about the Rosario search than three weeks of reading registers did.

Search for the people standing next to the person

This family is written Lerena, Llerena, Lorena, Serena, Lerina, Sancio, Gerena and Levena across the registers that hold it. A surname box cannot reach it reliably.

The parents can. Searching for a couple — father Sancho, mother María Fernández — rather than for their surname returned four of Sancho’s children in one query, including two the archive had never met. Godparents, spouses and witnesses work the same way: their names are spelled ordinarily, and they are standing in the same act.

Corrections

Corrections are the most valuable thing anyone can send. A family member with a date, a photograph of a document, or a flat contradiction of something written here will change the archive, and the change will be recorded rather than quietly made.

Two kinds of mark, and why both exist

A reader meeting a coloured chip should know which question it answers. There are two, and they are not the same question.

How well is it known?

Documented Inferred Family lore

Used on the narrative pages, against a claim. This is the vocabulary the Defranceski and Falco archives use too.

Is this person one of ours?

Connected Excluded Unplaced

Used in the register and on person pages, against a person. Most of the register is unplaced, and that is the point of the page.

A person can be documented and unplaced at once — well recorded, and not ours. Keeping the two marks separate is what stops a good record turning into a relative.

Living people

Living people appear by name only — no dates, no places, no photographs. That is the rule shared across this family’s archives, and it is stricter in practice than it sounds: a birth date and a city together identify a person completely.

Where a living person’s evidence bears on a dead one — a DNA test, a death notice they signed — the inference is published and the person is not. The DNA page → is the worked example.

This rule was broken until 10 September 2026, when the direct-line page was found to be publishing a living person’s exact birth date, two cities and both marriages beneath a caption claiming the details were withheld. Recorded on Corrections.

Every film number, tested

This archive cites 58 FamilySearch film numbers. Two Montevideo attributions had already failed inspection, which put every catalogue-derived number under suspicion. All 58 were tested on 20 September 2026. Fifty-seven resolve to a real film and not one points at a film that does not exist.

It was done with FamilySearch signed out, because the thumbnail endpoint is public. Two traps are recorded below, and both would have produced false corrections against perfectly good data — one of them caught me.

Two opposite forename traps

The two free sources this archive leans on hardest behave in opposite ways, and each punishes the habit the other rewards. Getting them the wrong way round produces a false null on either site, and a false null is the most expensive mistake this archive can make — it closes a door that was never shut.

Both were proved here with controls. On FamilySearch, an exact forename search matches the whole given-name string: exact Maria returns 53 Uruguayan Lerenas and Maria Lili Lerena is not one of them, while exact Maria Lili returns her alone. On FreeBMD the reverse holds — the printed index abbreviates the second forename, so Horace Archibald finds nothing while Horace A is sitting there.

SourceThe trap How it was provedWhat to do
FamilySearch, q.givenName.exact=on EXACT MATCHES THE WHOLE GIVEN-NAME STRING, not the first forename. A person indexed with two forenames is invisible to an exact search on the first one alone. Maria Lili Lerena, Uruguay. Exact 'Maria' returns 53 records and she is not among them. Exact 'Maria Lili' returns exactly 1 - her. Same surname, same country, same form; only the given-name string changed. Use the FULL given name exactly as the record is likely to hold it, or drop .exact on the forename and keep it on the surname. The fuzzy forename DOES match per token - fuzzy 'Agustina' returned hits on Augusto and Agustin - so fuzzy-forename plus exact-surname is the safe shape for a null.
FreeBMD and FreeCEN THE EXACT OPPOSITE. The printed indexes abbreviate the second forename, so searching both forenames finds nothing while the man sits there under an initial. Already logged by this archive: 'Horace Archibald' and 'William John' both returned nothing, and both men were present as 'Horace A' and 'William J'. Search the FIRST forename only. The habit that is correct here is the one that produces a false null on FamilySearch, which is exactly why the two are written down on one page.
THE BLAST RADIUS - 133 of the 278 people in this register carry two or more forenames - very nearly half. Any exact-forename null this archive has recorded against a two-forename person is unsafe and should be re-run in the fuzzy-forename / exact-surname shape. No such null could be identified by grep, because the searches were written down in prose without naming the parameter - which is itself the lesson: record the QUERY, not just the answer.
WHAT THIS DOES NOT UNDO - The central null on 'Juan Carlos Lerena' used the full string 'Juan Carlos' as the exact given name, which is the correct shape. It stands - but its stated limit is now sharper than when it was written: it would still miss a man indexed as 'Juan Carlos Maria Lerena' or as 'Juan C.', because the match is whole-string. The caveat on /direct-line/ said 'exact string'; this is what that costs.

Six trees agreeing, and one source

Six FamilySearch trees give Sancho Llerena the same parents — antoniojosed, rosanaic, jfina, nanabal, ladissosa and abal2018. That looks like strong corroboration and it is not corroboration at all. They descend from one published account — one of them carries in its own user name the surname that appears in that work’s acknowledgements — beside a single Spanish tree of undated estimates. Copies are not corroboration.

Documented This archive had already learned the same lesson from a parish book rather than a website: the three Francisco entries of 7 January 1846 that looked like three witnesses were one clerk, writing one page, on one morning. The shape is identical. Counting copies as witnesses is the commonest way an archive talks itself into confidence, and it happens on paper as readily as online.

Documented And where a census meets a reconstruction, the census wins. The two camps disagree about Sancho’s birth by thirteen years: the Uruguayan trees say 1767, antoniojosed says about 1754. The padrón of 1812 records him as 45, which is 1767. The 1754 is what you get if you assume a marriage in 1753 and a first child the year after — so that tree’s “about 1753” marriage is an estimate derived from a guessed birth, not a record of an act.

Documented Which dissolves a coincidence that looked real for an hour. A Lerena marrying an Aguilar at Córdoba in 1753, one year before a Sancho de Lerena y Armenta married an Ana María de Aguilar at Granada in 1754 — two families, two cities, one year apart. There is no coincidence, because the 1753 is not a date from a document. It is a tree’s arithmetic, built on a birth year the census contradicts.

Documented The opposite case is worth naming too: when an index disagrees with itself the way the acts do, the disagreement is in the registers. Dominga Josefa Lerena married a Capitán Don Antonio Ruiz — or Rodríguez — de Carvallo, and the two acts spell him differently. FamilySearch’s own index splits the same way. An index that reproduces a disagreement did not invent it.

What an información matrimonial contains, and why Sancho’s absence costs so much.

These petitions give the groom’s parents, his birthplace, and his years in the country — every one of the three things this archive cannot establish for Sancho Llerena. That is precisely why his would have been worth more than any other document named on this site, and why its absence is the expensive one.

And the gap where it should be is not quite final. The volume’s own filming-condition card reads “Volumen Entero: Manchas de Tinta … HOJAS SUELTAS” — loose leaves. It is a folder of separate sheets, not a bound book, so its order is the order somebody filed them in and is not guaranteed to be chronological. That is the one reason not to treat the January 1791 gap as absolutely final.

Documented And the same discipline run forwards, for once, rather than backwards. A parent-pair query on Félix Llerena and Rosalía Aguilar returned zero from Spain. The page rendered and reported a count, so it was not a blocked page. The control was run before the null was believed, not after: searching ciudad de Córdova and reading the collection names that came back.

They are Nobility Records — Granada 1489–1718 and Écija 1529–1907, Madrid notarial protocols, and induction files of the Orders of Chivalry. The only Córdoba in the whole corpus is Córdoba, VERACRUZ, in Mexico. So the full-text index for Andalusia holds nobility and notarial records and not parish registers: an eighteenth-century Córdoba baptism is not in the corpus being searched. The zero means “not findable by this instrument”, not “not there”, and it is recorded that way.

Inferred One more tier worth stating plainly. Fourteen records sit attached to Sancho Llerena in the tree, and the attachments are secondary — a tree user decided those fourteen are all one man, and the underlying acts have not been read here. One was opened to check and it is a proof of nobility of 1599, from Alcántara, Cáceres, Extremadura. A record can be perfectly genuine and still be attached to the wrong man; the attachment is the claim, not the document.
