Bideford, Northam — and a name that is a count

Mary Septima Taylor

She is buried in the Roman Catholic ground at Maitland under a Spanish surname she took at about twenty. Her own name is a piece of arithmetic, and it turns out to be exactly right.

The name is a count

Septima is Latin for seventh, and Victorian parents used such names literally. Count the daughters in order of birth — Kathleen, Isabella, Margaret, Irene, Irene's twin, Lucy — and Mary Septima is the seventh.

Inferred Which incidentally settles something the tree leaves blank: the unnamed twin of 1879 must have been a girl, or the arithmetic does not come out. The name her parents gave her ninth child records a sister whose own name has been lost.

Her granddaughter's generation kept it: an infant Antoinette Septima Lynette Lerena was born on 3 January 1943 and died four days later — a Johannesburg child, two generations and an ocean from Northam, still carrying Septima.

That is worth more than sentiment. This family named its children for their grandparents on both sides, consistently enough to be treated as evidence. This infant was the daughter of Roque, who was Mary Septima's son, so she was given her grandmother's name; and the same discipline put Juan Carlos — Pablo Armando's own father — into the name of her uncle, Ricardo Juan Carlos Lerena. Juan Carlos is the man the 1908 enrolment card names as Pablo Armando's father and whom no register anywhere has produced. His grandchildren were still being named for him in South Africa — which is not proof that he existed, but is evidence that the family believed he did, and knew what he was called.

The line behind her

Documented Above William Taylor stands James Taylor, born about 1796, and his wife Harriet Reynolds (1803–1827). Harriet died on 12 November 1827, when her son William was twenty-one months old. A first William Taylor had been born on 29 December 1824 and died the following September at eight months; the William who lived was given the dead child's name, as families then did.

That reaches back four generations above Mary Septima, to a man born in the 1790s — further than this archive has yet got on the Lerena side, where Pablo Armando's own parents are still unknown.

Northam and Bideford, north Devon

Documented Mary Septima Taylor was born at Bideford, Devon on 17 September 1884 — registered there, and christened on 30 October at St Margaret's, Northam, in the Anglican church.

Source: England and Wales Birth Registration Index, 1837–2008, citing the birth registration at Bideford, Devon, General Register Office, Southport. Held in the family's own tree.

Her father William Taylor was born at Worlingworth in Suffolk on 11 February 1826 and came west: he married Mary Ann Hellen Brayley at St Mark's, Bideford, on 26 January 1870, and their children were born and raised between Bideford and Northam — 23 Geneva Place, North Street, 2 Fore Street. He died on 30 November 1902 at 2 Park Terrace, Northam, of chronic bulbar paralysis, his son Horace Archibald present and giving the information; he lies in Northam churchyard, old grave XB28–K8.

Brayley is a north Devon name, from Bray. This archive inferred a Devon origin from that surname alone, before any place had been seen written down. The family's own records put them at Bideford and Northam — six miles apart, and both in Bray's country. The inference held.

A scholar

Documented On 1 December 1900 — six weeks after her mother's death, at sixteen — she was awarded the Bishop Phillpotts' Prayer Book Prize by the Exeter Diocesan Board of Education, in the general examination in religious knowledge for monitors, candidates and pupil teachers: Class III, monitors over sixteen and pupil teachers, one of forty-three out of eighty-eight who passed.

She was a pupil teacher — a girl of sixteen already teaching, and being examined on it. She signed her own name in a firm hand at her wedding eleven years later.

In the 1891 census, aged six, she was at 2 Crystal Villas, Stanton Road, Barnes, Surrey — living with an aunt, away from Devon.

The ship

Family lore The story is that Mary Septima and Pablo Armando met aboard a ship bound for South Africa.

The objection is a fair one and was raised by the family itself: she was in England, but he was an Argentine going from Argentina to South Africa — so what was he doing on a ship out of England? Three things make the story work better than it first looks.

1. The River Plate reached the Cape through Europe

In the 1900s there was no dense direct passenger service between Buenos Aires and Cape Town. The ordinary way to make that journey was to sail to Europe and change ships — Southampton being the hub of the mail service to the Cape. On that reading he was never "in England" in any meaningful sense: he changed ships there, and the leg on which they met was the Southampton–Cape Town run. "He came from Argentina" and "they met on a ship from England" are not in conflict at all. Inferred

2. The horses were already moving that way

Documented The South African War of 1899–1902 consumed horses on a scale never seen before: of some 519,000 horses used, about 360,000 were shipped into South Africa, drawn from England, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Burma — and Argentina. Contemporary British officers grumbled about the "mongrel Argentines" and set Australian bushmen to breaking them.

Pablo Armando Lerena was born in February 1882. He was seventeen when that war began and twenty when it ended — and he was a horseman, later an Argentine cavalryman and a Cape Town trainer. A young man of exactly his age and trade moving from the River Plate to the Cape in those years was moving with a trade, not against the current. The horses →

3. She had a documented reason to be aboard

Documented Her mother died in April 1900 and her father in November 1902. She was fifteen and then eighteen.

And from 1901 the Colonial Office and the emigration societies ran an organised programme to send single British women to South Africa — a scheme the war had turned from charity into imperial policy, aimed at securing the new colonies by settling British women in them. An orphaned Englishwoman of nineteen or twenty sailing for the Cape between 1903 and 1905 is not an anomaly. She is the type the scheme existed to carry.

What would prove it

All three of those are context, not evidence about these two people. The story is testable, and by a single class of record:

UK outward passenger lists (BT 27) survive from 1890 and are indexed by name. If Mary Septima Taylor and Pablo Armando Lerena sailed from Southampton for the Cape between about 1903 and 1905, both their names are on one list.

Their first child Roque was born at Cape Town on 25 November 1905, which brackets the crossing: they were both at the Cape, and married, by early 1905. Open questions →

They married six years after their first son was born

Roque was born at Cape Town on 25 November 1905. His parents married on 3 September 1911, and the register calls them bachelor and spinster — so neither had been married before, to each other or to anyone.

Inferred That closes a puzzle this archive had already noticed. Roque's baptism was delayed six years, to 12 June 1911 — and it was performed by Bertram W. Glynn, whose name then appears examining his parents' marriage register three months later. The family was brought into the church in the course of a single year: the child in June, the parents in September. Roque was born outside marriage and legitimated by it.

Set down plainly, because this archive sets things down plainly. It is also, on the record, a couple who stayed together until her death and a man who never remarried — a widower still on his passport in 1940 and on his death certificate in 1950.

She changed her church

Documented On 16 December 1911, three months after the wedding, Mary Septima was herself baptised a Catholic at Corpus Christi, Wynberg.

So the year runs: the son baptised in June, the parents married in September, the mother received in December. An Anglican girl from a Devon vicarage parish, christened at St Margaret's Northam in 1884, came into her Argentine husband's church at twenty-seven — and was buried from it, in the Roman Catholic ground at Maitland, twenty-four years later.

And it changes how the ship must be searched

The marriage does not disprove the crossing; they could well have met in 1904 or 1905. But it removes an assumption. She was Mary Septima Taylor until September 1911, so on any passenger list before that date she appears under Taylor, never Lerena — and, being unmarried, the two may have travelled separately and will not share a berth entry.

It also gives his trade at the earliest date yet: horse-dealer. The sequence runs horse-dealer (1911) → trainer (1940, and a yard taking apprentices in the 1940s) → race horse owner (1950). The horses →

The crossings, and a last voyage home

Six Board of Trade passenger records for this family, read in September 2026 and published here for the first time. They are ordinary shipping paperwork, and between them they hold the saddest fact on this page and one of the warmest.

Documented On 20 May 1935 Mary Septima landed at Southampton, aged 50, off the Balmoral Castle. Her address in Britain is given as “c/o Dalling, Fore St, Northam, Devon” — her elder sister Isabella Temple, who had married Henry Dalling at Northam in 1900. She was in England five weeks, and sailed for the Cape again on 28 June 1935 aboard the Armadale Castle.

She died on 15 November 1935, four and a half months after she got home. The section below gives the cause. Nothing in the paperwork says she knew, and this archive does not claim she did — but she was fifty years old, she had not been to Devon in the record since 1909, and she went back to her sister’s house in the parish she was born in one summer before she died.

Documented And the 1909 crossing corroborates the thing this page argues hardest. On 19 February 1909 the Guelph landed “Mrs M T Lerena” at Southampton, and on the line directly below her “Mast. L Lerena” — Master, a boy, almost certainly Roque Luis Armando, then three. She is travelling as Mrs Lerena with his son, two years before the wedding at St Dominic’s. The purser wrote her down as ENGLISH and the boy as BRITISH COLONIAL.

Inferred Then it happens a third time, a generation later. On 12 April 1957 Juanita Ramona Lerena — Roque’s daughter, Pablo Armando’s granddaughter, born 16 June 1937 — landed at Southampton, single, occupation BALLET TEACHER. Her address in Britain: “Vicosa, Westward Ho, N Devon” — the village adjoining Northam. Three generations of this family crossed to the same four square miles of north Devon: the daughter of the house in 1909 and 1935, and her granddaughter in 1957.

The Taylors had horses too

1891 and 1901 censuses of Northam, Devon.

This archive carried William Taylor — Mary Septima’s father — as a coachman. The censuses say something sharper. In 1891, aged 65, the enumerator writes his occupation as:

“Cab driver retired sergt p cavaloy” — cab driver; retired sergeant, cavalry

By 1901 he is a widower of North Street, Northam, and simply an army pensioner. His eldest son drove a cab beside him; his second son was a footman.

So the woman Pablo Armando married came from horses as well. The family story about him is entirely a horse story — trainer, breeder, stables on the Koeberg Road, four generations of jockeys after him. It turns out her father spent his working life in the saddle and on the box: a cavalry sergeant, then a cab driver in a small Devon town.

That is not evidence of anything, and it does not explain how they met. But this archive has spent a long time treating Mary Septima as the ordinary English half of the story, and that was plainly wrong. Horses stand behind both sides of this marriage.

An absence, recorded rather than explained. Mary Septima is not in the 1891 census. Born September 1884, she should be a six-year-old in that household between Lucy and Agnes. She is not there, and a search of the whole of Devon for a Mary Taylor born 1883–85 returns four girls, none of them her. She was probably away from home that night, or the index has mangled her — either is likelier than anything dramatic. But it is an absence, and it should be checked against the original image.

Her mother, Mary Ann Hellen, is recorded in 1891 as born in London, Middlesex — though Brayley, her maiden name, is a north Devon surname. Also worth resolving.

What she was doing at sixteen

Western Times, 4 April 1901, page 7 — repeated in the North Devon Herald a week later.

The Exeter Diocesan Board of Education examined the pupil teachers and monitors of Devon’s Church schools each year, and printed the results by name, age and parish. In April 1901, under Set II — monitors over sixteen, and pupil teachers in their first year, eighty-eight candidates were examined. In Class III, between a boy from Honiton and a girl from Honiton, sits:

“Mary Septima Taylor, 16, Northam”

She was training to be a schoolteacher. A pupil teacher stayed on at her own village school and learned the work in it, examined once a year, paid a little. It is the first thing this archive knows about her that is not a date.

And it sharpens the shape of her life to something quite startling. A pupil teacher at Northam in April 1901. “Mrs Lerena”, crossing from Cape Town to Southampton with a small son, by February 1909. Eight years, and the whole distance between a north Devon parish school and an Argentine horseman’s household at the Cape. What happened in those eight years is the largest thing still missing from her story.

The newspaper OCR is poor and the lists run together. Her own entry reads unambiguously in both papers; the class boundaries around it were reconstructed from a damaged column and should be re-checked against the image before anything is built on them.

The eight-year hole — and two ships that might close it

A pupil teacher at Northam in April 1901. “Mrs M T Lerena”, crossing from Cape Town to Southampton with a three-year-old son, in February 1909. Between those two facts lie eight years and six thousand miles, and they are, I think, the largest answerable gap left in this family’s story — larger than where Pablo Armando was born, and sitting in records that survive and are indexed.

The outward passenger lists give a clean negative first: no Mary Taylor of her age left Britain for South Africa at all, in the whole range 1890–1960. But the index frequently carries no forename. Drop it, and filter on surname, age and destination, and two candidates appear in exactly the right window:

Mary Septima was born on 17 September 1884. On 9 September 1903 she was eight days short of her nineteenth birthday — and the manifest records the passenger as nineteen. The occupation fits the life rather than the legend: a pupil teacher at sixteen, in a cab driver’s household of ten children, at a moment when the Cape was actively recruiting British domestic servants on assisted passage. A north Devon girl going out as a domestic in 1903 is not a romantic story — it is the ordinary one, and it would put her in Cape Town about two years before Roque was born there.

A candidate, not an identification Taylor is among the commonest surnames in England, and “Miss Taylor, 19” will have sailed for the Cape many times in those years. Two records fitting a window is not proof. The original manifests would show the forename — the index does not carry it, but the page usually does. Both references are given above.

Her brothers and sisters, taken off a tree

Until 14 September 2026 five of Mary Septima’s siblings were recorded here with husbands who had no first names — m. Dalling, m. Bell, m. Burton, m. Sweet, m. Wood — on the authority of a MyHeritage tree and nothing else.

That was a strange thing for this archive to tolerate. It has opened Montevideo parish acts from 1789 while carrying Devon marriages from the 1900s on a tree — and England is the best-documented jurisdiction it touches. All five husbands are now named, from the Devon parish registers, and it took under an hour.

Two sets of twins, and a wife who was not who the tree said

This archive had already noticed that Kathleen Georgina and William John were both born in November 1870 and written, against his name, “twins, or one date is wrong” — and then left it there.

The 1871 census settles it. Both are in the house as infants on 2 April 1871, and their parents’ banns were called at Bideford on 9 January 1870. Two children of one mother, both alive in a household fifteen months after the banns, cannot be separate births. They are twins — so the family had two sets, this pair and Irene’s in 1879.

And William John’s wife was not Fanny Ellen Jefferey. The 1901 census names her Fanny Helen, with children Gladys Helen and William Archibald; the 1911 census dates the marriage to 1892. No Devon record supports the surname Jefferey at all. The only Fanny Jeffreys to marry a Taylor in England married a James William Taylor at Dukinfield, Cheshire, in 1893 — a different man, county and father. Said as a suspicion and not as a finding: that looks like where a tree picked her up.

Her maiden name is therefore unknown again, which is a better position than a wrong one.

And the spelling of one name, three ways in two generations. Her mother-in-law’s own banns of 1870 call her Mary Ann Helena Brayley; this archive has carried Hellen; her daughter is Margaret Helen Walker in the parish book; her granddaughter is Gladys Helen. The name plainly mattered to them and nobody agreed how to spell it.

They moved to where a relative already was

In 1911 William John was living at Paignton, in south Devon. In 1909 his sister Lucy Elizabeth had her banns called at Churston Ferrers — three miles down the same coast, and eighty miles from Northam where they were both born. The brother was there first.

Irene shows the same thing in reverse. She married at Northam on 30 August 1900 and was living in Bermondsey, south London at the census of 31 March 1901, seven months later — and by 1911 the Burtons were back at Bideford, four miles from where she was born.

It is the same shape this archive keeps finding in Montevideo: people do not move at random, they move to where a relative already is. It is the first time the Devon side has shown it — and it bears on the open question of how Mary Septima came to be at the Cape at about twenty.

The naming runs the same way. William Archibald Taylor (1893) and Horace Henry Charles Dalling (c.1901) both carry their uncle Horace Archibald’s names, in two different households; Ronald William James Dalling carries his grandfather’s. That is the discipline that put Ricardo Juan Carlos on Pablo Armando’s eldest son, for a grandfather nobody can find. It runs on both sides of this marriage.

Kathleen, and a household nobody here had opened

This archive had only ever read the 1891 and 1901 censuses, both of the parental house. By 1911 William Taylor was nine years dead and the family had scattered — and the 1911 return gives five of Mary Septima’s nieces and nephews at one stroke.

At Northam: Henry Dalling, 32, a gardener (domestic) and five years younger than his wife; Isabella Temple Dalling, 37; and their children Horace Henry Charles (10), Ronald William James (8) and Sylvia Mary (0). At Bideford, the Burtons with Charles William and Albert Edward.

And in the Dalling house, entered as “Sister”: Kathleen Georgina Taylor, 41, single. This archive recorded her with no husband because a tree gave her none, which is evidence of nothing. Now it is evidence — the eldest of the ten, unmarried at forty-one, keeping house with a younger married sister.

Whether she married after 1911 is untested; she lived until 1940. A Find a Grave entry for a Kathleen Georgina Taylor at Brompton Cemetery in London, died 24 July 1942, was seen and not attached — two years out and the wrong city. It is recorded here so that the next person does not attach it either.

Still to do: the 1921 census, untouched, and the last before the 1931 returns were destroyed — by then Mary Septima was at the Cape and her sisters were not. Margaret Bell is not yet found in 1911; Charles William Bell is too common a name to search on.

The three addresses, and what a free census can reach

Three of the children were born at named addresses. An address is what a census is searched on, so these should place the household without a name search — and one of the three now does.

The other two were first written off as untestable. That verdict was wrong, and it was wrong in an instructive way: it tested the census route and was written as though every route had been tried. The UK National Archives had not been looked at once, and Geneva Place turns out to be a council school whose admission registers survive for 1885–1949. See The British Archives.

Neither of them went to sea

Northam parish contains Appledore, and the Royal Navy registers held no Taylor of this family — which left the merchant service open and looked like days of work, because crew lists are filed by ship and not by man.

It was not days of work. The 1891 census had already answered it, and had been in this archive since 16 September. William John, twenty, is a cab driver like his father; Horace Archibald, nineteen, is a footman in domestic service. Two brothers, two land trades, on one page — at exactly the ages a life at sea would have begun.

Did William John Taylor go to sea? 1891 census, his father's household at 2 Fore Street, Northam: 'William J TAYLOR, Son, Single, 20, born Bideford - CAB DRIVER'. His father, William Taylor, 65, is 'Cab driver, retired sergeant, cavalry'. NO. HE FOLLOWED HIS FATHER'S TRADE. At twenty he is driving a cab in Northam, which is precisely the age at which a seafaring career would already have begun. The ADM 188 null now has a reason behind it: he is not in the Royal Navy registers because he was not a seaman at all.
Did Horace Archibald Taylor go to sea? The same return: 'Horace A TAYLOR, Son, 19 - FOOTMAN, DOMESTIC SERVANT'. NO. In domestic service at nineteen. Two brothers, two land trades, on one census page.
So what would the crew-list search have cost? Crew lists are arranged by the ship's official number, not by name, so a man can only be found once his ship is known. For Bideford, Barnstaple and Appledore registry that is on the order of a hundred-odd vessels across twenty-five years, each with an annual agreement - several thousand documents, held in three places at once (TNA, the National Maritime Museum, and the Maritime History Archive in Newfoundland). Days of work, across three institutions, for a question the census answers in one line. Written down so that nobody reopens it as though it were merely untried.
And it could not have been done by name anyway The name-indexed registers of merchant seamen cover 1835-1857 and then resume with the Central Index Register of 1918-1941. Between them there is no surviving central register of seamen. The two men's whole working lives fall inside that gap. Even with unlimited hours there is no index that converts a name into a ship for the years 1885 to 1910, which is when either of them would have sailed. The route was never available, quite apart from being unnecessary.
What is left of the seafaring question - Closed for these two men, and only for them. The Taylors of Appledore on the Ulelia - T Taylor mate b.1846, J W Taylor master b.1888, T G Taylor b.1893 - are still joined to this family by nothing at all, and this finding does not touch them. If anything it widens the gap: a cab-driving, footmanning household in Northam looks less like a branch of a shipmaster's family, not more. Still not asserted either way.

Northam is in Devon

Documented In the 1921 census Lucy Elizabeth Sweet — 39, living at 23 Hamilton Street, Devonport — has her birthplace transcribed “NORTHAM, NORTHAMPTONSHIRE”. Northam is in Devon, two miles from Bideford, and it is where this family comes from. The transcriber met a place name that begins the same way as a county and finished the thought.

That is the second birthplace error in this one census, and it is worth recording as a search trap rather than a curiosity: anyone filtering a national index on Northamptonshire would collect her, and anyone filtering on Devon would lose her. A birthplace in a transcript is the transcriber’s reading of a word, not the household’s statement of a place.

The same entry carries something else. Lucy called her daughter Kathleen — for the eldest sister who never married and was an invalid in another sister’s house. The naming discipline this archive keeps finding in the Uruguayan acts runs just as plainly through a Devonport street in 1921.
