Rosario, Santa Fe — and the records that do not answer

Argentina

Rosario is the place the family names, and its records survive and are reachable. They have now been searched to exhaustion, and he is not in them — which is a finding, not a failure.

Confirmed, 8 September 2026

Pablo Armando Lerena was born at Rosario de Santa Fe on 22 February 1882, and was Argentine by birth, not naturalised — from the passport he carried, signed and thumb-printed. The page below was written before that document surfaced. The tradition it describes was correct. The passport →

What was claimed

Family testimony David Defranceski's great-grandfather — the grandfather of Cheryl Anne Lerena — came from Argentina, and Rosario is the city associated with him. He is said to have gone to South Africa as Consul General.

No name, no dates and no document are attached to this yet. Establishing the name is the first task of the archive, and everything else waits on it.

Why Rosario is plausible

Documented The name's distribution supports the story independently of the story. Argentina holds 293 Lerenas and Uruguay 431 — and Uruguay's density, 1 in 7,962, is the highest in the world. The River Plate is the modern heartland of the surname. A family tradition pointing to Argentina is pointing exactly where the name actually concentrates, which is a modest but real corroboration. The name →

Rosario itself fits the period. Argentina's second city and the great grain port of the Paraná, it grew through the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries on European immigration and on the pampas trade — cattle, grain, and horses. A family with standing enough to produce a consul, and interests in bloodstock, is a recognisable Rosario type of that era.

Uruguay is not a detour

The River Plate is one social world with a border through it, and families crossed it constantly. Given that Uruguay has the densest Lerena population on earth, and that the Argentine Stud Book's co-founder carried the Uruguayan double surname Lerena Lenguas, the archive treats Montevideo as a live possibility from the outset, not as a fallback after Argentina fails.

Stated plainly so it is not forgotten later: if the Argentine search stalls, the correct next move is Uruguay, not a harder Argentine search.

13 September 2026 — that instruction was followed, and it paid

The Argentine search did stall, and the next move was Uruguay. Both volumes of the Rosario baptism index were read end to end: one Lerena in a hundred and seventy years, and the act behind her says of both parents “naturales del E. Oriental”.

The measurement bears it out too. In the full-text corpus LERENA runs 6.4× commoner in Uruguay than Argentina, while its twin LLERENA runs 3.6× the other way — and two reference surnames measured the same way both favour Argentina. Lerena is the only one of the four that reverses, and it is the spelling this family used.

What it has not produced is Juan Carlos. The one documented Lerena household of that generation in the River Plate is now open from its marriage act to its last burial, and he is not in it. Hypothesis 1c → · What was read →

Where the records are

These are the seams to work, in the order they are worth working.

Civil registration, Santa Fe province

Argentina's civil registration begins in the 1880s. Rosario sits in Santa Fe province. Births, marriages and deaths from that point should name parents outright — the single most efficient way up a generation.

Catholic parish registers

Before civil registration, and alongside it, the parishes. Rosario's older parishes and the Archdiocese of Rosario hold baptisms, marriages and burials; many are filmed by FamilySearch.

Immigration and passenger records

The CEMLA immigrant database and the Argentine listas de pasajeros record arrivals into Buenos Aires with age, origin, occupation and often the town in Spain. If the family arrived from Spain in the emigration century, this is where the Iberian origin is named.

Outbound to South Africa

The departure matters as much as the arrival. A consular posting leaves a paper trail at both ends — see South Africa.

The consul: how it gets proved

A Consul General is a public officer, and public officers are published. This claim should be among the easiest in the archive to settle, once a name exists:

  • The Argentine Ministerio de Relaciones Exteriores published annual Memorias and diplomatic lists naming every consular officer and post.
  • The Boletín Oficial of the Argentine Republic records appointments.
  • On the receiving side, South African and British colonial government gazettes published the grant of an exequatur — the host state's formal recognition of a foreign consul. This is the crispest single document available, and it is dated.
  • Contemporary newspapers on both sides reported consular arrivals and social life. Argentine and South African digitised press are both searchable.

Note one caution to carry from the start: consuls were often honorary. A merchant resident in the host country could hold the post. If the great-grandfather was an honorary consul rather than a career diplomat, he may appear in the gazettes but not in the Argentine foreign-service lists — and that would also explain a man who bred horses and farmed ostriches while holding a diplomatic title. A hypothesis, recorded early so it can be tested

Uruguayan records already give Gilberto Lerena married to Julia Salvañach, with sons Carlos Augusto Federico (born Montevideo, 1883) and Raul (1885). Here they are, in Buenos Aires, ten years on — and with them Justiniana Lenguas, a widow of 73, which dates the death of Don Luis Lerena to before 1895, and a broker’s trade written against the head of the house.

Inferred So the co-founder of the Argentine Stud Book was a Uruguayan, born 1854 — which fits a Group 1 race being renamed in his honour in 1914, at or just after his death. And the children's birthplaces date the family's crossing precisely: Uruguay through 1887, Buenos Aires from 1889.

Documented A second Gilberto sits outside this household, and he is worth naming. On 27 February 1928 a Gilberto Lerena born 1879 landed at Buenos Aires aboard the Cap Norte, travelling as an Argentine national, a comerciante, accompanied by a son of the same name aged twelve. He was born within three years of Pablo Armando, and he is carrying the Gilberto forename of the Stud Book family above.

This page used to say he was “not in the 1895 household”. That was wrong, and it was wrong because of the table above. The table printed here until 20 September 2026 was built from the search index and held nine names. The schedule holds sixteen, and line 6 is “Salvanach Lerena, GILBERTO”, varón, 17, soltero — the eldest son, born 1877 or 1878, against the traveller’s 1879. The schedule had been read on 14 September; the page went on reasoning from the index for six days.

Inferred With that objection gone, the two records fit each other on every axis available: a birth year one apart, Argentine nationality — which the Salvañach son demonstrably holds, Montevideo-born though he was, since he appears in Argentine military records from 1911 to 1936 — Buenos Aires, a marriage of 19 May 1906 at the Pilar, and a son born 1916 who is exactly the estudiante of twelve walking down the gangway beside him.

They are deliberately not merged. Both records are index-level, and this archive once joined two Arturo Lerenas on a forename and an age and had to retract the branch within the hour.

Documented Half of that test was run the same evening, and the prediction it made came back true. If the traveller is the Salvañach son, then the son walking beside him should exist in a record. He does. Gilberto Lerena Salvañach married Cipriana Sáenz Peña at Nuestra Señora del Pilar on 19 May 1906 — a bride this archive did not have — and a Capital Federal parish index names their child Gilberto de Jesús del Corazón. His Argentine nationality stopped being an inference too: he is carried on Distrito Militar 2 in March 1922, born 1878 at Montevideo.

Inferred And it still does not close. That child has no indexed birth year — a search of Argentine Lerena births from 1910 to 1920 returns ten records and none of them is he, on a filter that demonstrably works, since it cut sixty-three results to ten. So the twelve-year-old estudiante of 1928 is still unmatched by a document, and a son who exists is not the same as a son who is that son. The two rows stay apart.

What the four charts say together

Three of them point back across the river and say so on the record — naturales del Estado Oriental, born Uruguay 1854, born Uruguay 1839. The Lerenas of Argentina are, so far as Argentina can show, Uruguayans who crossed.

The fourth points nowhere. Pablo Armando is documented in Argentina by a birthplace on his own passport and by nothing else: the Rosario baptism index holds no Lerena for 1881–83, the 1895 census holds no Lerena or Lerina household anywhere in Santa Fe province, and his parents exist on one card. He is the only one of the four whose Uruguayan origin is a hypothesis rather than a line of text. The two Uruguayan houses →

Three refusals, and one direction

Three independent bodies of evidence have now declined to put this family at Rosario:

Read

Fourteen years of cathedral baptisms, 1882–1895, line by line — and until 1888 there was no other parish in the city.

Searched

The whole FamilySearch index. Two records exist for him worldwide: a Cape marriage and a modern grave memorial.

Searched

The 1895 national census. No Lerena in Santa Fe; none anywhere answering to him.

Meanwhile Uruguay keeps answering: the world's densest Lerena population, a documented Montevideo kindred, and a household that crossed to Buenos Aires in the late 1880s. The hypotheses →

None of that unseats the passport. It is his own document, sworn at a consulate, naming a city, a province and a day. It does mean the corroboration has not been found where it ought to be — and that Argentine coverage online is thin enough for that to be a fact about the archives rather than about him.

Where to go next

Four routes, in the order they are worth trying. The first two are implied by the passport itself.

1 · His military enrolment — the strongest lead

The passport records a libreta de enrolamiento issued through the Consulado General Argentino at Cape Town, and gives his service as the Guardia Nacional, 6º de Caballería, 1915. Argentine military enrolment was made compulsory by Ley 4031 of 1901, and the libreta itself by Ley 8129 of 4 July 1911. A man born in 1882 belongs to the conscription clase 1882.

An enrolment record names the man's parents and his birthplace. Enrolment was done at the Distrito Militar of the declared domicile — so if he was at Rosario, Santa Fe's district holds it.

  • Archivo General del Ejército, Buenos Aires — takes reference enquiries by email.
  • FamilySearch has an agreement with the Argentine army archive; part of the collection is already online and should be searched before writing to anyone.
  • The Distritos Militares registers are listed by province by the Centro de Estudios Genealógicos de San Luis.

2 · The consulate that issued the passport

To be issued an Argentine passport in 1940 he had to prove nationality — which means the Consulado General at Cape Town held a file on him: a matrícula consular, the registration every Argentine abroad made. Those files went home to the ministry.

  • Archivo del Ministerio de Relaciones Exteriores y Culto, Buenos Aires.
  • And on the South African side, the consulate's own correspondence in the national archives.

3 · Civil registration, which is not online

Argentine civil registration is provincial, and Santa Fe's is not indexed on FamilySearch for the 1880s at all — a control search for a common surname at Rosario in those years returns nothing. It has to be ordered directly.

  • Registro Civil de la Provincia de Santa Fe — births, marriages, deaths.
  • Archivo General de la Provincia de Santa Fe, and the Rosario municipal archive.

4 · Uruguay, which the evidence keeps indicating

Montevideo civil registration and parish registers, and the Lerena Lenguas kindred already surfacing there. If Rosario continues to refuse, this is not a fallback but the likelier answer. The name →

And one question of its own. This family named a son Gilberto Lerena in 1957, in South Africa, three generations after leaving. The stud-book Gilberto is now a documented man with a documented household. Either they were kin, or they knew exactly who he was — and both are worth finding out.

The surname in Rosario, at last — but the wrong man and the wrong century

For a long time this archive could not find a single Lerena in the city, in any record, ever. Two searches have now changed that, and neither helps as much as it first appeared.

The 1869 national census. Bartolomé Llerena, 45, married, Argentine, born in Córdoba, working as a jornalero — Rosario, Sección 2ª. He is the only person of this surname found in Rosario before 1882, and he would be 58 the year Pablo Armando was born.

But the schedule was opened and read, and it deflates the find. Every line carries its own number, and the twelve people on the page are of a dozen different surnames — washerwomen, a laundress, labourers, a peon, a carpenter, a Spanish shopkeeper. It is lodgings, not a household. No wife appears beside him though he is recorded married, and no children. And he is a day labourer, not a horseman or a man of property.

The other two are twentieth century, at Santa Rosa de Lima — the parish that only opened in 1888: Luis Llerena and Clementina Baranda, whose son was baptised in 1903; and Manuela Lerena and Diego Román, whose daughter Carmen was baptised in 1918. Neither can be his parents. Both are on the register.

Which Rosario?

Uruguay has its own Rosario — a town in the department of Colonia, on the River Plate. This archive nearly recorded a Uruguayan groom as a “Rosario-born Llerena” linking the two countries, until his record was opened and every other person on it turned out to be Uruguayan: Solís Grande, Maldonado, Punta de Carretas, Cordón.

Given how much of this family’s evidence runs through Uruguay, that ambiguity deserves saying out loud. If any part of the family’s memory of “Rosario” came down through a Uruguayan branch, the town could be the wrong one — and years of work in Santa Fe would have been done in the wrong country.

Recorded, and weak The passport is against it. His own 1940 document says “Rosario de Santa Fe” — naming the province, not merely the town. It would take a great deal more than an ambiguity to overturn that.

The last three routes, walked

After the 1882 register walk and the test of the father's name, three routes were named as the only ones still live. All three were walked on 17 September 2026. None of them finds his parents, and that is written down here so the next person does not spend the same day finding out.

One of the three should not have been offered at all: in February 1882 Rosario had only one parish, so there are no "other parishes" to search. That is logged as a correction against this archive rather than quietly dropped.

The letter to the Registro Civil, and why the ask changed

The four results above all point off this site, to the provincial registry. Drafting the request changed it, though: civil registration in Santa Fe begins in 1886, four years after he was born, so the 1882 birth act this archive was about to ask for cannot exist. Asking would have spent the letter on a certainty.

So the letter asks for the three things that could exist — and the strongest of them is not about him at all.

The strongest ask, and it is not about him THE PARENTS' DEATH ACTS. Juan Carlos Lerena and María Lerena would very likely have died after 1886, so their deaths would be registered even though his birth was not. And an Argentine acta de defunción names the parents and the spouse of the deceased. That is a direct route to the generation above the wall, from records that should exist, and it is the reason to send this at all.
The second ask An inscripción tardía - a late birth registration. He held an Argentine passport and was enrolled in 1908, and both required proof of birth. With no baptism at Rosario across 750 read acts, something must have stood in for one. A late inscription is the obvious candidate and nobody has looked for it.
The fourth ask, which this archive values as much as a find A CERTIFICADO NEGATIVO. The province issues them, and the letter asks for one explicitly if the search fails. For an archive that records tested nulls, a negative certificate from the registry itself is not a disappointment - it is a document, and it is worth more than any number of unanswered searches.

The other archives

The Lerena Archive is one of seven, each following a different line of the same family. They use the same method and the same five words for how sure a thing is.

  • The Defranceschi Archive Istria · Kvarner · Carnia A family written down under eight sovereigns, in four languages, and spelled at least nine different ways. Defranceschi · De Franceschi · Defranceski · Defrančeski
  • The Falco Archive Arienzo, near Naples Nine documented generations born, married and buried in one valley, and then a migrant ship to Brisbane. Falco · Zampiello · Morgillo · Crisci · Liguori · Maione · Rivetti · Annecchino
  • The Blažević Archive Senj and the Krmpotska mountains An evidence-first archive for the family of Hedviga Blažević of Senj — carriers, a tobacco factory, and a wall grave. Blažević · Papić · Vukelić · Žubrinić · Kalanj
  • The Booyzen Archive Graaff-Reinet · Cradock · Johannesburg A Cape Dutch family that kept marrying British women, changed one letter of its name in a war, and came off the train at Johannesburg with nothing. Booyzen · Booysen · Kolbe · Barry · Downing · van Jaarsveld · Schoeman
  • The D'Arcy Archive England and Ireland · Parramatta · Brisbane Out with the 39th Regiment in 1825, and four generations afterwards in two Brisbane cemeteries. D'Arcy · Saniger · Sneyd · Hornby · Wakefield · Atkinson · Murdoch
  • The Mazza Archive Sicily and Calabria · Brisbane Four Italian quarters — Mazza of Piedimonte Etneo, and Prostamo, Arena and Polistena of Calabria — ending in four graves in a row at Nudgee. Mazza · Prostamo · Arena · Polistena
