What Has Been Read

An archive that publishes only its finds is advertising. This page lists every search made, what it returned, and what it cost — so that no one repeats a dead end, and so a null result can be read as evidence rather than silence.

Why a page of failures

Most genealogical work is negative. Knowing that a register has been read line by line and does not contain a name is worth as much as finding one — provided somebody wrote it down. Nobody ever does, which is why the same fruitless afternoon gets spent again a decade later.

So: everything below was actually looked at. Where a search failed, the reason is given — not there, not indexed, not digitised, restricted, or blocked. The distinction matters. A record that does not exist and a record nobody has scanned are different problems with different solutions.

The army enrolment drawer — the one that worked

Found 10 September 2026. Image group #112342450, Fichas de enrolamiento, Cajón 985 (Lera). Image 665 is Pablo Armando Lerena’s own enrolment card, naming his father and mother. The card →

It belongs on this page as much as on any other, because this archive had already found it and thrown it away. The machine index rendered him Armand Lorena, born at Rosario de Lerma, Salta, and the previous round wrote him off on four objections. Three were artefacts of the transcription. The image settled it in two minutes.

Images 650–688 of the same drawer were then swept through the viewer’s index panel. Five other Lerena families are in there — Gilberto, Alejandro, Arturo, Ramón, Carlos — and none is from Rosario de Santa Fe, and none has a father called Juan Carlos. No brother. That negative is recorded on the card’s own page, with what it does and does not prove.

The lesson, taken to heart. An index entry is a pointer, not a fact. Everywhere this archive has excluded a candidate on indexed detail alone, the exclusion is provisional until the image is opened. Those cases are being re-checked.

The index before 1879 — read end to end

13 September 2026. Eighty openings, a hundred and forty-eight years of one parish, and exactly one Lerena.

Everything read here until that morning began in 1879 — three years after Pablo Armando was born, which is fine for him and useless for everyone above him. The companion volume, film 004530324, carries a title card reading “Índice de Bautismos, Volumen 3º, Años 1731–1879, Letras L–Q, Revisado y Restaurado en 1945”. Same 1945 restoration, same typescript, same four columns.

It is where Juan Carlos Lerena’s own baptism would sit if he was born in the city, and where an older brother or sister of Pablo Armando would sit if the family were there in the 1870s. The L section runs from image 6 to image 85; image 86 begins the M section. Every opening between was opened and read.

The one name

LERENA LENGUAS, María Julia Margarita Josefa — 1866, Libro 15, folio 229.

Cross-indexed a line above under LENGUAS — the same child, the same folio. The 1945 typist entered her under both halves of her name, which is how we know the compound is real: a Lerena father and a Lenguas mother. Confirmed at full tile resolution before it was believed.

She is not Pablo Armando’s sister — sixteen years too early, and compound where he is simple. What she is, is the only Lerena baptised in the parish of Rosario between 1731 and 1901. Both volumes of the index have now been read end to end, and she is the sole occurrence in a hundred and seventy years.

Which sharpens the problem rather than solving it. Pablo Armando was born at Rosario on 22 February 1882 and his baptism is not in the index. That much was already known. What is new is that the family leaves no trace in the parish before then either. Whatever brought Juan Carlos Lerena and María to Rosario, they were not a Rosario family.

But a baptism at folio 229 has an act, and the act names both parents. It has not been read. If María Julia’s father is a Juan Carlos, or the father of one, the question moves back a generation in a single page. It is the first errand on the list.

The names that recur for a century and a half are Leguizamón, Lencina, López, Ledesma, Lescano, Ludueña, Lucero, Leiva, Luna, León, Luján, Llanos — hundreds of times each. Five near-misses were re-rendered at full resolution and recorded, so nobody has to check them twice: Lerones (1856); “Lesa (o Lera)” (1857), where the index itself offers Lera as an alternative reading; Loveras (1864); Lerré Echeita (1870), a Basque compound; and Lensina (1816), read at first glance as Leprina.

And then the act was opened

Bautismos 1865–1867, image 124 of 311, folio 229, act 1541. Image ark 3:1:S3HT-XS9C-ZL. Handwritten, read at full tile resolution in four overlapping renders. Born 8 October 1865; baptised 17 February 1866.

… Es hija legítima de Don Luis Lerena Lenguas y de Doña Julia Juanicó, naturales del E. Oriental; fueron padrinos Don Luis Lerena, representado por don Claudino Campos y Doña Margarita Mara de Carlos.

Natives of the Estado Oriental del Uruguay. The one Lerena in the whole of the Rosario baptism index is a Uruguayan, married into a Montevideo house, standing his own father as godfather by proxy — which is what people do when the godfather is on the other side of the river.

Which explains four negatives at once: no Rosario baptism for Pablo Armando, none for Juan Carlos, no Lerena marriage at Rosario, and no Lerena in the parish at all. A family that lives in a parish leaves baptisms. This one leaves nothing — because it was somewhere else. Hypothesis 1c →

No link has been shown between Luis Lerena Lenguas and Juan Carlos Lerena. Two men sharing a surname in one city sixteen years apart is a lead, not a relation.

The same night — she went home

FamilySearch’s full-text search reads handwriting across un-indexed images. It does not cover Santa Fe, which is why Rosario had to be read by eye. It does cover Montevideo. Given the phrase “María Lerena”, it returned a marriage register — and the image was opened rather than trusted.

San Francisco de Asís, Ciudad Vieja, Montevideo. Folio 364, act 1879.

Los tres de Noviembre de mil ochocientos ochenta y seis … el matrimonio que por palabras de presente contrajo Don Jaime Herrera, soltero, hijo legítimo del Doctor Don Nicolás y de Doña Adela Crucet, con Doña María Lerena, soltera, hija legítima de Don Luis Lerena y de Doña Julia Juanicó.

She is the child of folio 229. Born at Rosario 8 October 1865, baptised there 17 February 1866 — and twenty-one years old, in Montevideo, on 3 November 1886. The parents match by name in a second, independent, primary act, in another country, twenty years later. That lifts the Rosario couple from one act plus user-submitted genealogy to two primary acts: Luis Lerena and Julia Juanicó were a real Montevideo couple, and their daughter was born on the Argentine bank and married on the Uruguayan one.

The witnesses are the groom’s father and the bride’s mother. Luis Lerena is not at his daughter’s wedding; he may have been dead by 1886, which the act does not say and which is marked here as inference.

And the trap, said out loud. Pablo Armando’s mother is named on his 1908 enrolment card as María Lerena. This is a different María Lerena. She married Jaime Herrera in November 1886 — four years after Pablo Armando was born — and she was sixteen in February 1882. She cannot be his mother. A rare surname, a common forename and a strong wish for the pieces to fit is exactly how this archive has gone wrong before, so it is written down before anyone is caught by it.
What the frames are, all eighty-one of them. Images 35–42 are not index at all — they are the roll break: a target card, two blanks, “continúa en el próximo rollo”, “ARGN-0030 rollo 28”, “rollo 29”, “continuación”. Nothing is lost across the join; image 43 re-shoots image 34. Images 63 and 83 are duplicate exposures. That leaves seventy openings of actual index, continuous from 1731 to 1879, each one recorded by image, ark, years, libro and result.

The death index — opened for the first time

13 September 2026. The biggest untried record set in Rosario. It answers two thirds of the question and then runs out of pages.

Every Rosario sweep here had been for baptisms and marriages. Nobody had opened the deaths — and a death act does the same work as a baptism act: it names the parents of the deceased. If Juan Carlos or María Lerena died at Rosario, this is where they would be, and it would name their parents.

“Índice de defunciones 1800–1888”, eighty-six images, handwritten — not the 1945 typescript. And it is not one index but a composite, bound together:

1882–1888 (image 48): seventy-odd entries across seven years, including the year of his birth. Lugones, Ludueña, Lobos, Ledesma, Lucero, Leguizamón, Lescano, Lemos, Lejarza, Lazarte, Leahy, López, Llanes, León, Lencina, Lavallén.

And then the book runs out. The sequence that would cover 1840 to 1881 runs A, B, C, D, E, F — and the volume ends. There is no L section for those years in this film.

That is not a gap in the searching. It is a gap in the record, and it falls exactly where it hurts: a man and a woman who had a son at Rosario in February 1882 are most likely to have died in the decades either side of it, and for the four decades before it the finding aid does not exist. The gaps →

The death registers do survive to 1875 and are filmed, so such a death is still findable — act by act, the way the 1882 baptism register was read. That is a far larger job than an index would have been, and it is now the only route. After 1875 the collection holds no death register at all.

The register itself — carried to 750 acts, and the index vindicated

13 September 2026. The test of whether the 1945 index can be trusted at all.

Every Rosario null in this archive rests on a typescript index compiled in 1945 from the parish registers. Whether that typescript is complete had never been tested — and an index is exactly the kind of evidence this archive has been burned by before. The correction →

So the register itself was read, act by act. Every act carries the child’s name in the left margin in large script, so the two margin strips of an opening — tile columns 0–3 and 10–14 at full resolution — are rendered side by side in one image: one screenshot per opening, eight names at a glance.

Acts 113 to 862 — 1 February to 22 July 1882. Seven hundred and fifty baptisms. No Lerena, in any spelling.

Pablo Armando was born on 22 February 1882. That is the first two-thirds of his birth year, read from the register rather than the index.

And the index and the register agree. Across 750 consecutive acts, everywhere the index says there is no Lerena, the register says the same. He is not there — and the index is not the reason he is not there. That is a negative, but a load-bearing one: it means the Rosario nulls can be trusted, and the search belongs elsewhere.

Two near misses were logged and resolved rather than passed over. Armando Juan Rouillon (act 546, born 26 April 1882) — an Armando, two months too late and a Rouillon. And Pablo Jacinto Gauna (act 827, 11 July) — a Pablo whose margin surname was ambiguous at reading size and was re-rendered at full magnification specifically in case it read LARENA. It reads Gauna.

Five frames in the stretch are not new openings: three are duplicate exposures, and at two the filmer laid a loose certificate on the book, covering one page — each read from the facing frame. The volume ends at act 862; acts 863 onward are in the next one.

Where this stopped, and why

13 September 2026. Reading eighty openings of the pre-1879 index in an afternoon, and then the act behind the one hit, got this session firewalled by FamilySearch — the /search/ endpoints first, then the image browse. It is the second block in four days and both were caused by this archive’s own reading rate, not by anything FamilySearch did wrong. It lifts on its own.

What it blocks is route 1 of the roadmap: the Montevideo parish registers and Uruguayan civil registration, looking for a baptism for Juan Carlos Lerena in the 1840s or 1850s and a marriage to a María before 1882. That is the first place the evidence now points, and it is queued rather than done. The roadmap →

The anarchist press — answered from Amsterdam

10 September 2026. A letter sent on the 9th, answered on the 10th, testing the family tradition that he left Argentina as a communist on the run.

The International Institute of Social History holds the principal collection of the international left. Its Latin American periodicals are digitised and OCR’d as Latin American Anarchist and Labour Periodicals Online, c. 1880–1940. Their archivist searched it: “nothing popped up.”

The register itself — where that read began

10 September 2026. Not the index — the book.

Every null this archive holds for Rosario has rested on a typescript index: a later compilation, made from the registers, listing surname, forename, year and folio. It has been read exhaustively. But an index is somebody’s transcription, and whether the typist missed anything had never been tested.

So Libro 24 was read act by act. Each opening carries about eight acts, and every act has the child’s name written in the margin. All 183 margins in the span were read — not skimmed.

February and March 1882 are complete, act by act — acts 113 to 373, two hundred and sixty-one baptisms — and there is no Lerena in any of them.
He was born on 22 February 1882. This is the whole of the two months around his birth, and every margin has been read at a size where the name could not hide.

A correction to how the first pass was done. The first sweep rendered whole openings at 42% scale — legible enough to follow a page, not to be certain of a name. The proof came at act 312, where “Timoteo Lucero” had been read as “Armando Sara”: both words wrong, and an L-surname missed entirely — exactly the failure that matters when the whole exercise is the hunt for one L-surname.

So every opening was re-read from the margin strips at the film’s maximum resolution, rendering the two margin columns side by side in one image. The conclusion did not change; many individual readings did. The earlier readings should not be quoted.

What the book gave up along the way. Rosario cathedral baptised 112 children in January 1882 and 122 in February — about four a day. That is the first time this archive has had a rate, and it is what makes the sweep bounded rather than open-ended.

And a filming detail worth knowing: page 425 has a paper slip pasted over it, an annotation dated 29 May 1905, which on the film hides four acts. The next frame is the same page refilmed with the slip lifted, so nothing is lost. Whoever filmed this in 1974 was careful.

What it settles, and what it does not. The index is not hiding him — at least not here. For six weeks around his birth the register says exactly what the index says, and the two agree. That removes the most comfortable explanation: this archive can no longer say “perhaps the typist missed it.”

But six weeks is not a year. Infants were usually baptised within days or weeks, which is why that window was chosen — yet families did wait. A baptism in April, or in 1883, or at seven years old, is not excluded by this. What is established, from the book rather than from an index, is that he was not baptised at the cathedral in the six weeks around his birth. The rest of 1882 is about a hundred and thirty more frames: a long afternoon, not a mystery.

The marriage index — read again, at a resolution that leaves no doubt

10 September 2026. The enrolment card gave a father’s name; this is where his wife’s maiden name would be.

If Juan Carlos Lerena married at Rosario, the bride’s surname is in the Índice de matrimonios 1860–1904 — and that is the one thing the card could not settle. The registers had been swept before, but for a different question and at a resolution that had already produced one bad reading.

Unlike the baptism index, this one is handwritten. At the zoom the viewer allows, the initials are illegible and the surnames merely guessable — which is exactly how “Lavena” became “Larena” in an earlier session and kept a false candidate alive for two days. Read at full tile resolution, the L band is unambiguous.

No Lerena marriage at Rosario cathedral, 1867–1885.
Libro 6 through Libro 9 — the whole window in which a man whose son was born in February 1882 could have married.

The nearest approaches were checked at 1.4× to be certain: Lercari L. (1875), a Genoese name, and Llisalinos J. (1882). And the old error is now settled beyond argument — Libro 9, 1884, folio 20 reads LAVENA V. × BELMONTE A.

The L section, read end to end and closed

10 September 2026. The last open corner of the Rosario index, finished so it can be left behind.

The enrolment card changed the question. The earlier sweeps looked for the forename Pablo Armando under any surname. The card gives a father called Lerena, so the question became: does the surname appear in Rosario at all, in any year? Against that question the record had gaps — nothing recorded for 1879, 1880 or 1896–1900, and Ll read only for 1880–1884.

Every opening of the L section and of the Ll section has now been read, first to last, in order — 41 frames, 38 of them distinct and 3 duplicate exposures, each verified on arrival by year and Libro/folio, running continuously from Libro 23 folio 145 to Libro 48 folio 546.

No Lerena. No Llerena. No Larena. No Lorena.
Not in any year from 1879 to 1901. Not in either section.

The L section opens at 1880 — there are no L entries at all for 1879 — and runs past 1900 into 1901. The entire Ll section, across twenty-two years, comes to twenty-six entries. The nearest thing to the name in either section is LLERA: Pedro, 1898, and Ida C., 1900.

And the Armandos. The forename this archive has argued is rare does exist at Rosario — four times in the L band, and never in the right year: Lofré (1889), Luján (1895), Lunardi (1895), Limonetti (1897). All after 1889. Armando looks like a name that came into fashion at Rosario in the 1890s — which makes a boy christened Pablo Armando in February 1882 slightly unusual for his date, and slightly more likely to have been named for someone.

The method, which is the part worth keeping. The index is typewritten, not handwritten — the obstacle was never the hand, it was the viewer, whose zoom would not go far enough to read a page. FamilySearch serves each page as DeepZoom tiles, and level 13 for this film is 20 × 15 tiles of 257 px — about 5100 × 3800 for a two-page opening. Assembling those tiles into a plain HTML grid at 42% puts a whole opening on one screen with every surname legible. Image numbers drift between viewer states and are not to be trusted; the image ark is stable, and that is what the data file records.

What it settles, and what it does not. It settles that the surname is not in the Rosario cathedral index — absent, across twenty-two years, in a section read end to end. Until 1888 the cathedral was the city’s only parish. It does not settle where he was baptised. Three possibilities remain, and this archive should stop calling it a null and start calling it a question: he was not baptised as an infant; he was baptised outside the cathedral’s books; or the index itself is incomplete — it is a later typescript compiled from the registers, not the registers. That last is the one nobody likes to say aloud, and it is why an act-by-act reading of the 1882 register is still worth doing one day.

What the null result proves — and what it does not

The cathedral was the only parish in Rosario until 1888, and civil registration in Santa Fe did not begin until about 1886. So this index was the single most likely record of his birth anywhere. It rules out one specific thing, cleanly: no child named Pablo Armando was baptised at Rosario cathedral in 1882, under any surname at all.

It does not rule out four other possibilities, and it is worth being exact about them. He may have been baptised under a different given name — he was Robert Paul in South Africa and Pablo Armando only on the passport, and the sweep looked for that pair. He may have been baptised in a different year, if the passport's 22 February 1882 was wrong or approximate. He may have been born somewhere else entirely and only later associated with Rosario. Or he may never have been entered in this register at all.

The second of those has now been tested and closed: 1881 and 1883 are read in full, and he is in neither. So the Rosario baptism route is exhausted, not merely unsuccessful, and it should not be reopened without a new reason. What remains open is the first, third and fourth — a different given name, a different place, or no baptism recorded here at all.

The weight now falls on evidence gathered elsewhere, which grew stronger while this sweep ran: that he held Argentine nationality by 1911, proved from outside the family; that the surname’s kindred sits at Trinidad, in Flores, Uruguay, where civil registration began in 1879 — three years before his stated birth; and that the family’s political-refugee story, if it means expulsion under the Ley de Residencia, would require him to have been foreign-born, which would explain this null result completely. The cheapest untried test is now Uruguayan civil registration for 1882, not another parish register.

Spain

FamilySearch: Spain Baptisms 1502–1940, Catholic Church Records 1307–2005, Civil Records 1475–2005, and the provincial municipal registers.

The last of the four candidate birthplaces, and it had a real claim: 888 Lerenas in Spain — the largest absolute total anywhere — and the CEMLA arrivals cluster hard on Logroño, La Rioja.

The whole of Spain yields one Pablo Lerena of the right generation: Pablo Timoteo Lerena Muñoz, born 1878 at Bochones, Atienza, Guadalajara, son of Pedro Lerena Pérez and Baltasara Muñoz Bermejos. Four years early — and he married at Atienza and stayed. He is not our man.

But the useful result is the other one. There is no Armando Lerena in Spain at all. Not one, in any collection, in any century — every hit is fuzzy noise. And Armando is the name this family keeps: Pablo Armando; his son Roque Luis Armando; Anton Armando of the 1980 divorce and the 2013 estate; and Gregorio Armando Lerena, marrying at Flores in Uruguay in 1930.

Armando is a Río de la Plata name, not a Spanish peninsular one of the 1880s. It entered Spanish usage through Italian influence — precisely the demographic signature of Buenos Aires, Rosario and Montevideo in the late nineteenth century, and not of rural La Rioja. Across three whole years of the Rosario index there were two Armandos: rare, but present. Across four centuries of indexed Spanish records, none.

So the name points away from Spain and back across the Atlantic. That is not proof — but it is the first evidence in this project that discriminates between the candidate origins rather than merely failing to confirm one.

The Spanish bearers are also scattered rather than a kindred — Almería, Écija, Barcelona, Valencia, Valladolid, Navarra, Guadalajara, Huelva, León, Ferrol — and in most of them Lerena is the second, maternal surname: Manuel Fernández Lerena, Juan García Lerena. It behaves like a name being absorbed into other families, not one founding them. Nothing like the six brothers at Trinidad.

The honest limit: this is a weaker negative than the Uruguayan one. Spanish parish registers are enormous and only patchily indexed, and diocesan archives hold far more than has been keyed. An 1882 baptism in an unindexed Riojan parish would not surface here. What can fairly be said is that he is not in the indexed Spanish records, and the given name his family carried does not belong to Spain.

What the failures add up to

Three independent bodies of evidence decline to place this family at Rosario: fourteen years of cathedral baptisms, the whole FamilySearch index, and the 1895 national census. And there is no civil fallback, because provincial registration did not yet exist.

That is not nothing. It means his South American origin rests entirely on his own passport — a document he swore at a consulate, naming a city, a province and a day. The record has not contradicted him. It has simply, so far, refused to agree.

If you have looked somewhere this page does not list, that is worth knowing too. Corrections and additions are the most valuable thing anyone can send.

Searching the rarest name, not the surname

13 September 2026. The most useful thing learned here in a week, and it was learned by publishing a confident negative and being wrong within the hour.

Luis Eugenio Lerena Lenguas died somewhere between July 1886 and July 1910. Two sweeps were run for him and written up on this page as honest nulls: 1,502 Montevideo Lerena deaths for 1880–1911, and 132 Luis Lerena deaths for 1899–1903. Neither contained him. This archive went further and framed the FamilySearch Tree’s date of 1901 as a lead that the registers appeared to contradict.

The registers did not contradict it. The index did. FamilySearch files him as Luis SERENA Lenguas. Every search made on the surname missed him, and would have gone on missing him for as long as anyone kept searching that way. He died on 24 December 1901; the register’s own margin reads LERENA LENGUAS, LUIS.

He was found by searching his mother. This family is written, by the Montevideo clerks and the FamilySearch indexers between them, as Lerena, Lorena, Serena, Llerena, Languas, Lenquas, Loupuan and Guanico. The surname is the least stable thing about them. Justiniana Lenguas is rare, and rare names survive transcription because a clerk with no expectation of them writes what he hears.

The working rule, now written down. For a family whose surname is unstable, search on the rarest stable name in the household — a mother, a grandmother, an unusual forename — and not on the surname. And more generally: when a search fails, suspect the index before concluding the record does not exist.

That one search returned more than the death. The same eight rows gave four siblings of Luis Eugenio — and Gilberto Lerena among them, which is how the co-founder of the Argentine Stud Book turned out to be a brother of the man whose daughter was the only Lerena baptised at Rosario in a hundred and seventy years. The surname is the worst way to find them

14 September 2026. Three days of searching Lerena, and the surname turned out to be the least reliable handle on this family. It has now hidden records three separate ways.

How it hidWhat was missed, and for how long
A misspelt index Luis Eugenio’s death is filed by FamilySearch under “Luis SERENA Lenguas”. Every search on the surname walked past it — including a 1,502-row sweep of Montevideo Lerena deaths run the same morning, which this archive then published as a negative. A rank read as a name The 1842 marriage act calls the bride’s father “el Sr. Gral. D. Pedro”. The index renders that “Geraldo Dios Lenguas” — a searchable person who never existed, made out of a military rank.
A compound surname This archive swept the Montevideo dispensation register on the formula “Lerena feligresa”, which assumes the parish follows the surname. Ema Sixta is written “Doña Ema Sixta Lerena Juanicó, feligresa de la metropolitana”. The phrase cannot see her. Every child of both Montevideo couples carries a compound surname, so the sweep was blind to precisely the people this archive most wanted.

The working rule, now written down. Search the compound surname, the mother’s name, and the rarest stable word in the household. Treat the bare surname as the search of last resort, not first. And when a search fails, suspect the index before concluding the record does not exist.

Searching the compound surname “Lerena Juanicó” for the first time returned thirty-five Montevideo documents and two findings in one afternoon: that the sixth child, José Julio, sat as a Juez de Paz y Oficial del Estado Civil of the first section of Montevideo from at least 1902 to 1914 — and a ninth Lerena marriage dispensation, his sister’s, that the archive’s own sweep of that very register had missed.

The dispensation file’s headline conclusion — that no Lerena–Lerena dispensation exists in seventy-six years, and every Lerena in it marries out — has not been withdrawn. But it now rests on a search demonstrably blind to compound surnames, and it is marked as needing a re-run rather than left standing as though nothing had happened.

ALEX, the Austrian legal archive — asked for, and answered honestly

David asked, in passing, whether anything on alex.onb.ac.at could relate to the Lerenas. It is a fair question to put to any large digitised corpus, and it deserves a real answer rather than a shrug.

What ALEX is: roughly five million pages of Austrian and Habsburg official text — the Reichsgesetzblatt 1849–1918 in ten languages, the Staats- and Bundesgesetzblatt, the provincial Landesgesetzblätter, stenographic parliamentary records, and the Hof- und Staatshandbuch.

Does it hold a Lerena? Unknown — and not determinable, because its full-text search does not work. Every query returns the same stub. That was established the way this archive now establishes every null: by running controls. Konsul, Uruguay and Montevideo each returned an identical 166-byte response. A word certain to appear in five million pages of Austrian law returning zero is not a null result; it is a broken search.

Retested on 14 September by submitting the site’s own form as a POST rather than the GET used the first time — a stricter test, since a malformed request could have explained the first result. The form comes back unchanged. The title search works and the volumes are online; it is the full-text layer that is down.

And the honest answer on relevance, independent of the outage: the prior is low. This family is Uruguayan, Argentine and Spanish, then South African and British. Nothing in the record touches the Habsburg lands. ALEX is a law library for a state these people did not live in.

There is one real hook, and it is consular. Whether Pablo Armando was a Consul General is a standing question here, and consular appointments are gazetted. Austria-Hungary kept consulates at Montevideo and Buenos Aires, and the Hof- und Staatshandbuch lists consuls. If any Lerena held an Austro-Hungarian honorary consulship in the River Plate, ALEX could show it — and the family’s standing is exactly the class honorary consuls were drawn from: a president at a font, a general, a judge, an abogado. It would not show Pablo Armando’s own claimed consulship, which was Argentine and at the Cape; for that the gazette that matters is the South African one, already on this archive’s list.

A note that belongs to a different archive. ALEX is likely to be far more use to the Defranceski side — Istria, Kvarner and Carniola were Habsburg territory, and the Landesgesetzblätter are the right body of law for them. That is this project’s sibling archive and the two are kept strictly apart; the suggestion is recorded here only so it is not lost.

This is an independent negative, by a different method. Everything this archive knew about his absence came from reading the Rosario baptism index end to end for 1731–1901 and the register act by act for February and March 1882. Full text reaches documents no index covers, and it reaches them by name.

What it does not mean. Full text covers a fraction of what is filmed; the machine mangles this surname into at least seven forms, only three of which were tried here; and a baptism act often names the child by forename alone in the sentence that matters. It means no transcribed document in that corpus puts Pablo Armando next to any spelling of this surname. That is worth having, and it is not proof of absence.

And the two Juan Carlos hits are both namesakes, checked and excluded. One is Juan Carlos González Lerena, dispensed the conciliar proclamas at Montevideo in the 1920s — Lerena is his mother’s surname, the same shape as the only Juan Carlos Lerena in all of Geneanet. The other was born in 1910, baptised at San Nicolás de Bari in Buenos Aires, son of Rafael Llerena. Neither can be a man who fathered a son in 1882. Both are in the register so that the next reader who runs this search does not spend an hour excluding them — and Rafael Llerena, Argentine-born about 1881 and living at Calle Cerrito 535, is himself the kind of person this archive has been looking for.

Two false positives, and a failure that would have faked the answer

Image 64 fired on “Serena” — the exact S-for-L this archive has documented five times. The entry reads “hijo leg. de José y Lorenza Serena”. But the entry above it reads “José y Lorenza Merina” and the one below “José y Lorenza Jesusa”. Three consecutive siblings, one mother, and the machine guessed her surname three different ways. Not a Lerena.

Image 71 fired on “Solerina” — a different surname that happens to contain the letters of one being searched. The pattern was too loose, and was tightened to whole words afterwards.

And the one that mattered

Seven images returned a zero-length transcript on the first pass — and every one of them read clean on retry. An empty transcript is not “no Lerena on this page”; it is “this page was not read”. Had they been counted as clean, this page would be claiming a complete read of a register with seven unopened pages in it.

That is the third time in one day this source has failed by returning something rather than by erroring — after the results page that renders “(0)” while still loading, and the invalid place facet that answers a confident zero. The rule now extends: a reading loop must validate its own output, and a page counts as read only when its transcript is non-empty.

Rosario — the confirmations, opened for the first time

A confirmation act names the parents, and Pablo Armando was born in February 1882. The children confirmed at Rosario in March 1890 are aged six to fifteen, so he is squarely in range — and his parents, Juan Carlos Lerena and María Lerena, are the whole question this archive exists to answer. The register — Confirmaciones 1883–1918, film 1091419, 421 images — had been listed as untried since the archive’s first week and had no film number until 13 September 2026.

Documented It cannot be skimmed. Each entry reads “<number> <given name>, de <age> años, hijo/hija leg. de <father> y <mother>” — and the surname is never written. It has to be read out of the father’s name, so unlike the baptism registers this one cannot be scanned for Lerena down a margin. Every line must be read.

Documented The 26 March 1890 sitting runs to at least 243 children, and there is no Lerena in it. One sitting confirmed well over two hundred. A second sitting followed that June and July, with the numbering restarted at 1.

Documented There is a Pablo, and he is not ours. Entry 138 is “Pablo (11), hijo leg. de Manuel Cobos y Mercedes Alvarez”. Eleven in 1890 is a birth about 1879, and the parents are wrong. It is recorded here because it is exactly the hit a future reader will find and have to spend an hour excluding.

Inferred The population is heavily Italian — Vaccaro, Balestrini, Rossi, Cambursano, Massari, Calcaterra — which is Rosario in 1890, and which cuts the way this archive’s standing argument already runs: the Lerenas were not a Rosario family.

A mistake made and corrected the same day, and it is the more useful half.

From the first eleven entries this archive recorded that “every entry on this page is a girl … if the confirmations were taken sex by sex, a boy of eight would be in a separate run of pages, and that is where to look for him.” That is wrong. Entries 133, 135 and 138 are Domingo, Santiago and Pablo — all hijo leg. de, boys, mixed straight in among the girls.

The first eleven happened to be girls and a structure was built out of a sample of eleven. Had it stood, it would have sent the next reader to look for Pablo Armando in a separate run of pages that does not exist.

The other archives

The Lerena Archive is one of seven, each following a different line of the same family. They use the same method and the same five words for how sure a thing is.
